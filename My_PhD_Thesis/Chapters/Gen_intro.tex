\section*{Background of the study}

Novel antimalarial therapies are required to combat drug-resistant \emph{Plasmodium falciparum} (Pf) and help with malaria elimination. Malaria is one of the deadliest disease afflicting the mankind, with \num{282} million cases and over \num{610000} reported deaths globally in 2024, concentrated in the WHO African Region (\emph{World Malaria Report} 2025\footnote{World Health Organization} \cite{who_malaria_2025}). The disease poses a risk to half of the world's population, with non-Mediterranean Africa and Southeast Asia being particularly affected \cite{Alghamdi2024}. Malaria continues to represent a significant public health challenge across the African continent, particularly in regions situated south of the Sahara Desert, which accounted for $94\%$ of malaria cases and $95\%$ of malaria deaths in 2023. Notable reductions in incidence and mortality were observed between 2000 and 2022; however, the disease continues to disproportionately affect children under five, who represent a significant malaria fatalities. Key countries, including Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Tanzania, and Niger, contribute substantially to the global mortality burden. In Cameroon, the entire population is susceptible to infection, with the highest risk concentrated in the equatorial and coastal regions. As one of the 11 countries with the highest malaria burden, Cameroon accounts for $2.6\%$ of all global malaria cases and $2.1\%$ of malaria deaths. The transmission of malaria is particularly pronounced in forest, coastal, and humid savanna regions, with the rainy season (July to October) contributing to an escalation in risk due to increased rainfall and temperature. The nation faces a multitude of interconnected challenges, including drug and insecticide resistance, climate change, and disparities in healthcare access, which collectively impede efforts to control malaria transmission by infected Anopheles mosquitoes. Moreover, malaria imposes a substantial economic burden, manifesting in increased healthcare expenditures, diminished productivity, and stunted GDP\footnote{Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is defined as the total market value of all goods and services produced within a nation's borders over a specific period. The fluctuations in GDP over time are typically measured on an annual or quarterly basis.} growth. This underscores the imperative for ongoing public health interventions and the development of novel control strategies.

In essence, a drug is any substance that has the capacity to cure or prevent diseases in humans and animals. For many centuries, drugs were derived from natural sources \cite{Schedler2006}. These substances were often discovered by chance, and their mechanisms of action were not always understood. Moreover, they were not always capable of being synthesized industrially. The process of discovering drugs was largely based on serendipity, or, in other words, fortunate accidents. The seminal contributions of Pasteur, who proposed the concept of a drug as a molecule with a biological mechanism of action, were informed by the preceding observations of van Leeuwenhoek. These observations, which included the identification of microorganisms and their association with diseases, laid the foundation for the field of microbiology. Indeed, at the beginning of the 20th century, Ehrlich articulated in \emph{"Principle of Chemotherapy"} that an appropriate dose of a chemical could disrupt the growth of disease-causing microorganisms \cite{Alizon2018}. This "principle" became a reality in the 1930s when Fleming discovered penicillin. Subsequent to this breakthrough, considerable efforts were made to synthesize or isolate compounds analogous to penicillin, which resulted in the development of various antibiotic families. Beginning in the 1950s, significant progress in the field of human genome research, complemented by substantial advancements in cell and molecular biology, paved the way for the development of rational drug design methodologies. In this novel approach, biologists formulate informed hypotheses concerning the mechanisms underlying diseases. Consequently, the process of drug discovery underwent a shift from a primarily chance-based approach to a more systematic one. This transformation occurred as researchers began to link diseases with specific proteins present within biological pathways, thereby facilitating the development of targeted organic compounds for therapeutic interventions. The objective of rational drug discovery is the identification of disease-associated targets and their ligands \cite{Kubinyi2003}. Consequently, in response to the AIDS epidemic that emerged in the 1980s, extensive research efforts culminated in the development of combinatorial chemistry, a field that facilitates the synthesis of a vast and diverse array of organic compounds. These compounds can then be tested for their efficacy in combating specific diseases. In the contemporary era, although chance still plays a role in the process of developing new drugs, recent technological advancements and discoveries have rendered the drug development process more rationalized \cite{Galloway2010}.

The process of drug discovery can be defined as the identification of novel pharmaceutical compounds with the potential to treat diseases \cite{Paul2010,Hughes2011}. In essence, this process entails the identification of novel chemical entities that possess specific chemical properties for the treatment of diseases. This process is often characterized by a significant investment of resources, both financial and temporal, and involves a rigorous testing and experimentation phase aimed at identifying drugs that are both safe and effective \cite{Schaduangrat2020,Sliwoski2014}. The primary objective of a scientist in the realm of drug discovery is the identification of lead or hit molecules of the highest quality. However, it is imperative that these lead molecules not only exhibit pharmacological activity but also ensure safety for both humans and the environment. Subsequent to the identification of a promising lead molecule, a series of trials and assessments are conducted to predict its pharmacokinetic properties, including Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity (ADMET) characteristics \cite{Schaduangrat2020,Lin2003,Naithani2024}. The process of developing new drugs is costly and time-consuming, necessitating the testing of thousands of chemical compounds and conducting numerous experiments to identify drugs that are both safe and effective. In light of these challenges, there is an imperative to establish robust, standard, and reproducible computational methodologies to achieve the objectives set. These computational approaches are instrumental in determining the characteristics of receptors and compounds, thereby facilitating the binding process \cite{Li2021}.

Despite progress in reducing malaria-related morbidity and mortality over the past two decades, the constant emergence of parasitic resistance to current antimalarial drugs has made the discovery of new drug candidates a major global health priority \cite{Neves2020,Raphemot2016}. Currently, artemisinin-combined therapy (ACT) since their implementation in 2006 remains the treatment of malaria, but Pf-resistance cases were recorded also. The regional picture has hardened: partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives, mediated by mutations in \emph{Pf}kelch13, is now confirmed or suspected in at least eight African countries \cite{artemisinin_partial_resistance}, spatial-temporal modelling indicates that the geographic footprint of \emph{pfk13} markers on the continent is expanding \cite{young2026artemisinin}, and genomic surveillance documents the co-occurrence of \emph{Pfcrt} and \emph{Pfdhfr} resistance variants across diverse transmission settings \cite{letebo2026surveillance,global_resistance_landscape_2026,pfk13_resistance_2026}. Malaria is known to have a large global burden, with strong poverty-promoting effects. It persists as chronic infections, in spite of the available effective medical treatments. While aggressive control measures have reduced prevalence rates in some regions, the emergence of drug resistance, particularly to artemisinin-based combination therapies, complicates treatment protocols and undermines elimination efforts. Therefore, the urgent need for innovative antimalarial compounds and therapeutic targets has become paramount \cite{Mathews2018}. In this context, understanding the sociocultural dynamics of health care partnerships in endemic regions can improve the effectiveness of interventions \cite{Kraeker2013}. Taken together, these challenges underscore the importance of sustained investment in research aimed at overcoming these barriers and achieving long-term malaria control.


Natural products (NPs) and their structural analogues have historically made significant contributions to pharmacotherapy, particularly for infectious diseases such as malaria. A notable example is  \emph{artemisinin}, a breakthrough treatment derived from \emph{Artemisia annua} (sweet wormwood), which was recognized with the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015. However, natural products pose considerable challenges in the realm of drug discovery, primarily due to technical obstacles during the screening, isolation, characterization, and optimization processes. These challenges have contributed to a marked decline in the pharmaceutical industry's engagement in natural product research from the 1990s onwards \cite{Atanasov2021}. NPs exhibit distinctive characteristics when compared to conventional synthetic molecules, thus presenting both benefits and difficulties in the context of drug discovery for malaria. These compounds exhibit a remarkable diversity in their scaffolds and structural intricacy. NPs characteristically exhibit higher molecular weights, a greater number of sp3 carbon and oxygen atoms, and a lower number of nitrogen and halogen atoms in comparison to synthetic libraries. They also feature increased numbers of hydrogen bond acceptors and donors, demonstrate lower calculated octanol-water partition coefficients (indicating greater hydrophilicity), and exhibit enhanced molecular rigidity compared to synthetic alternatives. These distinctive properties can prove advantageous in the development of antimalarial drugs. For instance, the increased rigidity of NPs is valuable when targeting protein-protein interactions in Plasmodium parasites. Notably, natural products represent a significant source of oral antimalarial medications that function beyond Lipinski's rule of five \cite{Lipinski2004,Lipinski1997,Lipinski2004a}. The increasing molecular mass of approved oral medications, including antimalarials, over the last two decades demonstrates the growing importance of compounds that do not conform to this conventional pharmaceutical rule \cite{Shultz2018}.

Machine Learning (ML) have significantly advanced drug discovery. Pharmaceutical companies have greatly benefited from the utilization of various ML techniques to discover or re-purposing novel drugs candidate. The various ML algorithms have been used for predicting chemical, biological, and physical characteristics of compounds. Deep Learning (DL) techniques have become exemplar methods for drug activity prediction, target discovery and lead molecule discovery \cite{Patel2020}. These algorithms have been extremely used alongside the vast data generated utilizing high throughput sequencing to enhance the target discovery process. High throughput screening (HTS) using either approach entails screening of a large library of compounds. This process is often inefficient and not cost-effective because a high failure rate at subsequent stages of drug discovery. 

Biopharmaceutical industries are focusing on computational approaches in order to enhance the drug discovery processes as to reduce research and development expenses by diminishing failure rates in clinical trials and ultimately generate superior medicines. Drug discovery using ML techniques can be divided into four major steps: \emph{(i)} identify and curate datasets (potential target), \emph{(ii)} build and validate models, \emph{(iii)} score and prioritize a drug library, \emph{(iv)} validate predictions \emph{in vitro} or \emph{in vivo}. Different ML approaches help to identify drugs targets, find suitable molecules from data libraries, suggest chemical modifications, etc. The application of ML and Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods to drug discovery are reviewed in several recent papers. DL methods have made a massive impacts in science and technology generally, and in drug discovery particularly. Ashdown et \emph{al.} \cite{Ashdown2020} set out to develop a high-throughput cell-based, drug screening method that defines cell morphology and drug action from primary image data. From the outset, they sought to encompass asynchronous asexual cultures of the human malaria parasite Pf., by designing a semi-supervised DNN strategy that can analyze fluorescence microscopy images. The model can quantify the effects of antimalarial drugs both in terms of changes in life history distributions and morphological phenotype or drop points not observed during asexual development. Keshavarzi et \emph{al.} \cite{KeshavarziArshadi2020} introduce DL based process (\emph{DeepMalaria}), capable of predicting the anti-Pf inhibitory properties of compounds using a graph based model and their SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry Specification) \cite{Weininger1988,Honda2019}. Monika Samant et \emph{al.} \cite{SamantMonika2016} have developed a protein-protein interaction network of Pf. and human host by integrating experimental data and computational prediction of interactions using the interlog method. To complement the medical professionals, Qazi Ammar et \emph{al.} \cite{Arshad2021} have devised a DL based method to automatically detect/localize the Pf. parasites in the photograph of stained film. They introduced a new large scale microscopic image malaria datasets, where cells are tagged from the microscopic images. Their model are able to classify Malaria life-cycle classification in thin blood smears images. Traditional machine learning approaches for drug discovery encounter computational limitations when processing complex molecular data, which is the stated motivation for quantum machine learning as an alternative. The claim that this alternative can achieve exponential speedups with relatively modest quantum resources is a hypothesis rather than an established property, and it is evaluated in this thesis against classical baselines on a fixed chemical space. The real question is, with all the modern technological advancements in drug discovery, how can we utilize innovative technologies to find new active compounds more efficiently, thus reducing the cost?

Quantum machine learning (QML) is an emerging field that integrates the principles of quantum computing with machine learning algorithms, and it is evaluated here as a hypothesis about computational capability rather than as an established one. This approach integrates the principles of quantum physics with the adaptability of machine learning, thereby offering new possibilities for solving complex problems across various domains \cite{Suzuki2020,Mensa2023}. QML has been proposed for pharmaceutical development across enhanced molecular representation \cite{Roy2023,McKinseyCompany2023}, sophisticated feature extraction methods \cite{Huang2021}, accelerated screening processes \cite{Biamonte2017}, improved structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis capabilities \cite{Cao2019}, optimized lead compound management \cite{Outeiral2020}, refined predictions of drug-target interactions \cite{Smaldone2024}, and streamlined handling of complex biological data \cite{PerdomoOrtiz2017,Cerezo2020}; whether any of these proposals survives a strong classical baseline on antimalarial chemical space is an empirical question this thesis answers. The majority of QML applications in drug discovery are primarily focused on the initial stages of the drug discovery pipeline, particularly with regard to the identification of novel drug-like molecules. The complexity and intricacy of QML pose significant challenges in the interpretation of results, thereby hindering the identification of the molecular mechanisms that underpin drug-likeness \cite{Bova2021}. The utilization of QML models necessitates high-performance computing resources and specialized software \cite{Cerezo2023,Britt2015}.

Quantum computing is hypothesized to accelerate certain drug-discovery tasks, such as wavefunction simulation and combinatorial optimization, through exponential Hilbert-space scaling. These tasks sit in the initial phases of drug discovery, such as the identification of novel drug-like molecules and the prediction of molecular properties \cite{Batra2021,Avramouli2023,Avramouli2022,Batra2020}. Whether this hypothesis holds for molecular property prediction on finite-size antimalarial panels is an empirical question this thesis addresses. Quantum approaches to drug discovery are widely motivated by the prospect of accelerating the research and development phase and reducing costs, with some studies proposing that quantum simulations could shorten the R\&D phase to mere months and decrease costs to a fraction of those incurred by traditional methods \cite{Wong2023}; whether any such reduction is realized on real molecular prediction tasks remains an open empirical question, and no time-or-cost reduction is claimed by this work. QML frameworks, including those employing quantum support vector classifiers, have demonstrated superior performance in predicting the ADME-Tox properties of molecules compared to classical models \cite{Bhatia2023}. This enhancement is critical for informed decision-making within the pharmaceutical industry \cite{Bhatia2023}. A primary challenge in applying QML to drug discovery is the necessity of compressing extensive datasets of molecular descriptors to align with the capabilities of quantum computers, necessitating innovative data handling and algorithm design approaches \cite{Batra2021,Bhatia2023}. The amalgamation of quantum computing with machine learning is a complex endeavor requiring interdisciplinary collaboration. The integration process involves addressing technical challenges related to algorithm development and hardware limitations \cite{Avramouli2022}. Despite the potential benefits, QML remains in its early stages, and its practical application in drug discovery is constrained by the current state of quantum computing technology. Hybrid solutions that combine classical and quantum methodologies are presently recommended to capitalize on the strengths of both \cite{Avramouli2023}. QML techniques, such as generative adversarial networks and variational auto-encoders, are being investigated for the generation of small and large drug molecules and for virtual screening processes. These applications can improve the efficiency of identifying potential drug candidates \cite{Mensa2023,Li2021a}. Claims of quantum advantage in drug discovery, particularly in ligand-based virtual screening and other computationally intensive tasks, remain to be demonstrated against strong classical baselines, and this thesis treats them as hypotheses under test rather than as expectations.

\section*{Statement of the problem}

The state of the art reviewed above converges on a single representational crisis with four interdependent facets, and together they define the gap this thesis addresses.

The crisis is this: classical molecular descriptors systematically fail to extrapolate across stereochemically complex African natural-product scaffolds because they encode two-dimensional topology, meaning atom-connectivity graphs, rather than three-dimensional geometry, meaning spatial coordinates, bond angles and stereocentres. The expansion developed in this thesis quantifies the challenge before any model is trained: \qty{92.6}{\percent} of the expanded molecules are novel at the whole-molecule level while \qty{69.3}{\percent} of the seed scaffolds are retained, so the novelty lies in peripheral substitution on conserved ring systems --- precisely the variation that hashed two-dimensional encodings flatten. The crisis manifests in four empirical facets.

\emph{The scaffold paradox (chemical-space facet).} African-natural-product-inspired libraries exhibit high whole-molecule novelty coexisting with high scaffold retention, on rigid polycyclic frameworks with multiple stereocentres. Classical fingerprints describe local atom environments with hashed bit collisions, fixed dictionaries, or bounded topological distances, and hash both the novel periphery and the conserved core into the same feature space, collapsing stereoisomers into identical bit vectors. None of these encodings was designed for the structures that dominate this chemical space, and the question of what that costs downstream is opened here.

\emph{The estimand-divergence problem (validation facet).} Computational antimalarial prioritization rarely treats resistance explicitly: candidates are ranked on wild-type docking scores or on predicted activity against current strains, and the mutant states that drive treatment failure enter the analysis, if at all, as qualitative structural discussion. Static docking scores and short explicit-solvent molecular-dynamics trajectories disagree on the direction of resistance-mutant effects in \qty{87.5}{\percent} of matched states, yet both are used interchangeably in prioritization pipelines. What each method actually measures has never been formalized on this chemical space, and neither the dynamics-based stress test nor the retrospective comparison against published genotype-resolved inhibition data that would bound the static score has been performed here.

\emph{The quantum-inspired null result (descriptor facet).} Whether the descriptors proposed for exactly this purpose --- topological invariants, tensor-compressed embeddings and quantum feature maps --- actually improve activity prediction on such a panel is an empirical question that has not been answered for this chemical space; the literature that motivates them reports their geometric properties, not their benchmarked performance against a well-maintained classical baseline on the same data. This thesis answers it, and the answer is null: the simulated quantum kernel matches a bandwidth-tuned classical kernel (\num{0.823} against \num{0.829}, $p = \num{0.060}$) despite a fourfold higher kernel--target alignment, and topological or tensor fusions add no predictive gain over the extended-connectivity fingerprint. The quantum-inspired designs inherit the information ceiling of the fingerprint they consume: garbage in, garbage out.

\emph{The learned-representation collapse (extrapolation facet).} Graph neural networks and molecular transformers learn their own representations, yet under Bemis--Murcko scaffold separation the graph isomorphism network reaches \num{0.8047} against \num{0.8300} for the fingerprint baseline, and the deficit is largest exactly where structural novelty is greatest. Local message passing aggregates neighbourhoods on a two-dimensional graph; it cannot propagate stereochemical information beyond its receptive field, which is the failure mode the scaffold paradox predicts.

These four facets share a common root cause: no representation examined encodes stereochemistry, bond polarity or three-dimensional geometry directly. Hashed fingerprints project three-dimensional molecules onto fixed two-dimensional dictionaries. Local message passing cannot propagate stereochemical information beyond its receptive field. Quantum kernels fed by fingerprint-derived inputs cannot create three-dimensional information ex nihilo. Whether structure-direct quantum encoding --- placing molecular structure into quantum feature space from the outset, so that the circuit itself is the representation --- overcomes these blind spots is precisely the question that a genuine quantum machine learning implementation must answer on this chemical space, and it has not been answered.

A fifth, methodological consequence ties the four facets together: they cannot be adjudicated without an evaluation protocol that the literature does not provide. Published comparisons between molecular representations are difficult to compare with one another because each uses its own panel, label source, partition family and multiplicity handling, and several of the most-cited positive results were obtained under random splitting, which the representation-learning literature itself identifies as flattering to learned models. A benchmark that reports one split family, one label source and no power analysis cannot distinguish a genuine representational advantage from an artifact of panel composition. What is missing for antimalarial chemical space is a frozen, fully declared evaluation protocol under which classical and non-classical representations can be compared with the same data, the same splits and the same statistical machinery --- which is what this thesis builds and uses for every result it reports.

\section*{Research questions}

This thesis investigates a single overarching question through four empirical stages.

\emph{Central question:} can structure-direct quantum molecular encoding overcome the stereochemical blind spots and scaffold-extrapolation failures that limit classical, quantum-inspired and learned molecular representations on African natural-product chemical space, and what must such an implementation demonstrate before any hardware-based advantage claim?

The six research questions follow the four-stage empirical arc of the thesis; each stage consumes the result of the one before it.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Stage I (chemical space and validation).} Does an African-natural-product-inspired generative expansion produce a library that is genuinely novel at the whole-molecule level while retaining the scaffolds of its seed set, and can target-anchored docking on such a library support a resistance-aware prioritization layer?
	\item \emph{Stage II (estimand architecture).} Do static docking scores and short explicit-solvent molecular-dynamics trajectories estimate the same quantity across a resistance-mutant panel, and if not, how should the disagreement be used in a triage pipeline?
	\item \emph{Stage III (representation-ceiling diagnosis).} Do topological, tensor-compressed and quantum-inspired descriptors add predictive value over an extended-connectivity fingerprint baseline for antimalarial activity prediction on a frozen panel?
	\item \emph{Stage III (representation-ceiling diagnosis).} Do graph neural networks and molecular transformers close the gap to classical fingerprints when evaluation enforces structural extrapolation through scaffold-separated partitions, and what structural cause explains the failure if they do not?
	\item \emph{Stage IV (structure-direct quantum encoding).} Do the stereochemical blind spots and scaffold-extrapolation failures of hash-based fingerprint prioritization persist when molecular structure is encoded directly into quantum feature space?
	\item \emph{Stage IV (evaluation standard).} What evaluation standard must a genuine quantum machine learning study satisfy on this library before any hardware-based claim can be made?
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Aim and objectives of the study}

The aim of this thesis is to determine, under a single frozen evaluation protocol on African natural-product chemical space, whether quantum, quantum-inspired and learned molecular representations improve antimalarial candidate prioritization over strong classical baselines, and to establish what a genuine quantum machine learning implementation must demonstrate on this library.

The specific objectives, corresponding to the four empirical stages, are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Stage I (chemical space and validation).} To construct an African-natural-product-inspired hybrid library and characterise its chemical space by target-anchored docking against four mechanistically distinct antimalarial proteins.
	\item \emph{Stage I (chemical space and validation).} To validate the docking protocol by redocking and an external benchmark, declaring the estimand boundaries of static scoring upfront.
	\item \emph{Stage II (estimand architecture).} To develop a resistance-resilience scoring layer on the docking campaign and stress-test it with explicit-solvent molecular dynamics and a retrospective genotype--phenotype confrontation.
	\item \emph{Stage III (representation-ceiling diagnosis).} To benchmark topological, tensor-compressed and quantum-inspired descriptors against classical fingerprints under a frozen evaluation protocol with declared power boundaries.
	\item \emph{Stage III (representation-ceiling diagnosis).} To benchmark graph neural networks and a molecular transformer against the same baselines under scaffold-separated evaluation, diagnosing the structural cause of any failure.
	\item \emph{Stage IV (structure-direct quantum encoding).} To test whether structure-direct quantum molecular encoding overcomes the stereochemical blind spots and scaffold-extrapolation failures identified by the preceding benchmarks, against classical three-dimensional descriptors as well as fingerprint baselines.
	\item \emph{Stage IV (evaluation standard).} To define the comparison standard, data, partitions and statistical machinery that the quantum machine learning programme must satisfy before hardware evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Significance of the study}

The significance of this work is threefold.

Methodologically, the thesis delivers the first frozen, fully declared benchmark of classical, topological, tensor and quantum-inspired molecular representations on African natural-product chemical space, with scaffold-grouped partitions, declared label provenance and stated power boundaries. Its negative and null results are documented with the same machinery as its positive ones, which makes them usable: any future quantum implementation on this library inherits a comparison standard rather than a collection of incomparable one-off studies.

For antimalarial research, the resistance-aware layer contributes a concrete, reusable workflow: a corrected wild-type transporter receptor, a ratio-based retention score with a declared eligibility rule, a static-to-dynamic divergence diagnostic with its sampling limits quantified, and a retrospective phenotype check that bounds what the static score can claim. The workflow is deliberately protocol-local, but its components, particularly the WT-eligibility gate and the estimand-divergence framing, transfer to other resistance-relevant target panels.

For the wider drug-discovery community, the honest-negative record assembled here addresses a documented reporting gap: positive representational results are easy to publish and hard to reproduce, while careful null results, which are what actually bound a field, are rarely reported with the detail needed to build on them. This thesis reports its boundaries at the point of each result, so that the quantum machine learning programme that follows starts from measured limits rather than from aspiration.

\section*{Organization of the thesis}

This thesis is organised into three chapters, followed by a general conclusion.

\Cref{Chap:1} presents the literature review. It begins with the biological and clinical background of malaria and its drug-discovery landscape, then covers natural products as a source of antimalarial chemistry, machine learning in drug discovery, the fundamentals of quantum computing and quantum machine learning, and the computational drug-discovery methods on which the thesis builds: molecular docking, molecular dynamics, chemical-space exploration, resistance-resilient scoring and polypharmacology. It closes with molecular representations for machine learning, generative models, integrated validation workflows, and a synthesis that positions the research questions of this thesis within that landscape.

\Cref{Chap:2} presents the tools and methods. It specifies the construction of the hybrid library, the four-target docking protocol and its validation, the resistance-resilience scoring framework with its estimand architecture, the molecular-dynamics protocol and its three cohorts, the classical, topological, tensor and quantum-inspired descriptors, the machine-learning architectures, and the statistical framework, including partition families, multiplicity handling, power boundaries, uncertainty taxonomy and the claim-ledger convention used throughout.

\Cref{Chap:3} reports the results and discussion as a single empirical arc, study by study. It covers the chemical-space construction and target-anchored docking results, the resistance-aware scoring results including the static-to-dynamic estimand divergence and the retrospective phenotype confrontation, the quantum-inspired and topological representation benchmark, and the learned-representation benchmark under chemical distribution shift; it then reports the structure-direct quantum encoding programme as work in progress --- its archived exploratory phase, its frozen encoding substrate, its pipeline confirmation and its predeclared benchmark --- before closing with a synthesis that states the comparison standard the quantum machine learning programme must meet.

The general conclusion summarises the findings, discusses their limitations, and suggests avenues for further studies, with the quantum machine learning programme as the principal forward direction.

\section*{Overview of the thesis argument}

The empirical question at the centre of the thesis can now be stated precisely: under a single frozen evaluation protocol on African natural-product chemical space, do quantum, quantum-inspired and learned molecular representations improve antimalarial candidate prioritization over strong classical baselines, and what must a structure-direct quantum implementation demonstrate before any hardware-based advantage claim? The question is deliberately narrow and empirically answerable. It is addressed through five studies that share one library, one set of activity labels and one evaluation protocol. The chemical-space study constructs an African-natural-product-inspired hybrid library of \num{65856} molecules and characterises it by target-anchored docking against four mechanistically distinct antimalarial proteins, together with a per-target resistance-resilience score. The resistance-scoring study converts that docking campaign into a resistance-aware triage framework and then stress-tests it with explicit-solvent molecular dynamics, which reveals that static docking scores and short trajectories are non-equivalent estimands rather than interchangeable measures. The representation study evaluates the descriptors that motivate the quantum framing, namely a persistent-homology topological fingerprint, a Tucker-compressed tensor network embedding and a simulated quantum kernel, against an extended-connectivity fingerprint baseline and a bandwidth-tuned classical kernel. The learned-representation study extends the comparison to graph neural networks and a molecular transformer under scaffold-separated evaluation, where the effect of chemical distribution shift on model ranking becomes measurable. The quantum machine learning programme, the only part of this thesis that uses genuine quantum machine learning, encodes molecular structure directly into parameterized quantum circuits through quantum molecular structure encoding, which places bond-order and Coulomb-adjacency information into one- and two-qubit rotation angles and trains a hybrid quantum-classical model end to end; its canonical benchmark is not yet computed, and no comparative result from it is reported here. Its reformulated benchmark question, stated in advance of the results, is whether this structure-direct encoding surpasses classical three-dimensional descriptors --- extended three-dimensional fingerprints (E3FP), ultrafast shape recognition with pharmacophoric atom types (USRCAT) and three-dimensional autocorrelation functions (Autocorr3D) --- on the high-complexity quartiles of the African-chemotype structural index under scaffold separation. The programme is presented as the comparison standard that the completed studies define rather than as a source of results, and its central question is whether the two blind spots this thesis documents, stereochemical collapse in hashed fingerprints and the scaffold-extrapolation deficit of learned models, are addressable by an encoding that never passes through a fingerprint at all.

The organising finding of the thesis is negative and is stated in advance so that it cannot be mistaken for a later qualification. Across every representation tested, ECFP4 with a random forest remained the strongest descriptor for activity prediction on the panels studied; no fusion with topological, tensor-compressed, graph-based or sequence-based representations improved on it; and the quantum kernel matched, but did not beat, a well-tuned classical kernel. The completed studies therefore establish three things that a quantum machine learning study should be expected to respect: a well-characterised chemical space in which resistance and breadth are separable properties, a set of classical baselines that are demonstrably hard to beat under scaffold-separated evaluation, and a set of diagnostic quantities, particularly from persistent homology, that describe molecular structure without predicting activity. The chapters that follow develop that argument from the biological and methodological background through to the results, and they report null and negative findings with the same detail as positive ones.
