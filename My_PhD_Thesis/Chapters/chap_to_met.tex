\chapter{Tools and Methods}\label{Chap:2}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:ch2_intro}

This chapter presents the computational methodologies, algorithms, and protocols employed throughout this thesis to investigate quantum machine learning approaches for antimalarial drug discovery. The work encompasses five interconnected studies, each addressing a distinct aspect of the computational drug discovery pipeline: a chemical-space construction and multi-target docking study on an African natural-product-inspired library; a resistance-aware scoring study that validates its predictions through explicit-solvent molecular dynamics; an evaluation of quantum-inspired molecular descriptors against classical representations; a benchmark of graph neural networks and transformer models against classical fingerprint-based approaches; and a quantum machine learning programme that extends these benchmark findings toward true quantum machine learning, in which molecular structure is encoded directly into parameterized quantum circuits and evaluated on near-term quantum hardware; the corresponding protocols and results are developed in the chapters that follow.

Each study adheres to rigorous provenance standards, ensuring that all computational predictions are clearly distinguished from experimental validations. Null and negative results are reported with the same transparency as positive findings, reflecting a commitment to scientific integrity.

The chapter is organised as follows. \Cref{sec:chemspace_methods} specifies the construction of the hybrid library, its two complementary expansion routes, the scoring terms of the prioritisation pipeline, and the selection of the four protein targets with their structural preparation and the correction of the transporter receptor. \Cref{sec:docking_methods} details the docking protocol, its three-part validation by redocking, external benchmarking and positive-control enrichment, the consensus arm, and the multi-seed and perturbation audits. \Cref{sec:rrs_methods} establishes the resistance-resilience scoring framework: the estimand architecture, the score definition with its eligibility rule and class table, the network and chemical-space descriptors, and the retrospective genotype--phenotype confrontation. \Cref{sec:md_methods} describes the molecular-dynamics protocol, its three cohorts, the trajectory analysis, the endpoint estimation and the interaction-fingerprint complement. \Cref{sec:descriptors_methods} presents the classical fingerprints and the topological, tensor and quantum-inspired descriptors, including the conformer-generation governance and the kernel diagnostic hierarchy. \Cref{sec:ml_methods} covers the random-forest baselines, the graph and transformer architectures. \Cref{sec:stats_methods} closes with the statistical framework: partition families, activity-label provenance, performance metrics with their prevalence-imposed ceilings, conformal calibration and the distance-aware triage rule, significance testing with its power boundaries, the uncertainty taxonomy, the reproducibility infrastructure and the claim-ledger convention used throughout the thesis.

\section{Chemical Space Construction and Virtual Screening}\label{sec:chemspace_methods}

\subsection{Hybrid library construction}

The chemical space exploration aims to identify structurally diverse antimalarial candidates by combining African natural products with synthetic antimalarial chemistry and expanding around both seed sets. The seed collection comprises \num{396} African natural products and \num{454} synthetic antimalarial compounds. Canonical SMILES strings are used as the molecular identity, and duplicates are removed after canonicalisation so that every entry in the library corresponds to a unique structure.

Expansion proceeds through two complementary approaches. First, the Cheese API performs a similarity search with Morgan fingerprints (radius \num{2}, \num{2048} bits) at a Tanimoto threshold of \num{0.7} against the ZINC15 and ENAMINE-REAL collections, retrieving \num{100} analogues per seed. Second, STONED-SELFIES generates \num{100} variants per seed through single-character SELFIES mutations drawn from an alphabet of size \num{50}, which explores substitutions that a similarity search cannot reach. Generated structures are filtered for pan-assay interference compounds, validated with RDKit and Open Babel, and deduplicated by canonical SMILES, yielding a hybrid library of \num{65856} unique molecules of which \qty{94.1}{\percent} are Lipinski-compliant. Chemical novelty relative to the African natural-product seed set is quantified by the maximum whole-molecule Tanimoto similarity computed with the same Morgan parameters; molecules whose maximum similarity falls below \num{0.4} are treated as whole-molecule-novel. Because the two expansion routes act on different parts of the molecule, the library was additionally characterised by scaffold recovery, computed as the fraction of annotated seed Bemis--Murcko scaffolds detected in the expanded set.

Prioritisation of the expanded library proceeds in two stages. A SMILES variational autoencoder provides a continuous latent representation of the library, and $k$-means clustering of the selected \num{64}-dimensional representation yields \num{484} centroids. This centroid-based sampling reduces the cost of the subsequent docking campaign from exhaustive docking of every molecule to docking of the cluster representatives, while preserving structural diversity through the representatives themselves. Multi-parameter optimization then combines docking-derived scores, DiffDock confidence, the quantitative estimate of drug-likeness, an ADMET composite, and a Lipinski penalty. The \num{76} centroids above the optimization threshold map to \num{53} clusters and expand to \num{40481} molecules, of which \num{19913} remain after synthetic-accessibility and multi-parameter filtering. The \num{19913} molecules are computational hypotheses rather than confirmed synthetic targets.

The generative stage has declared failure modes that the filtering stage is designed to absorb, and naming them clarifies what the library is. SELFIES-based mutation guarantees chemical valence validity by construction, but validity is not plausibility: single-character mutations can produce strained ring systems, reactive functionality or fragmentated structures that a chemist would not recognise as analogues. Similarity-based retrieval produces plausible analogues but inherits the scope of the retrieved catalogue, so structures absent from the commercial collections are unreachable by that route. The two routes therefore cover complementary failure profiles, and the PAINS filter plus valence and standardisation checks remove the portion of both outputs that would corrupt downstream scoring. The scaffold-recovery statistic closes the loop: because it measures how many annotated seed ring systems survive expansion, it detects the failure mode in which expansion drifts away from the medicinally relevant framework entirely, a failure that whole-molecule novelty statistics cannot see because they reward exactly that drift.

The individual scoring terms of the prioritisation pipeline are declared explicitly, because each carries its own estimation assumption. The quantitative estimate of drug-likeness (QED) weights eight physicochemical desirability functions (molecular weight, octanol--water partition coefficient, hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor counts, polar surface area, rotatable bonds, aromatic ring count and structural alerts) into a desirability score in $[0, 1]$\cite{Bickerton2012}. Synthetic accessibility is estimated with the SYBA Bayesian classifier, which scores a molecule from its fragment contributions without requiring a retrosynthetic search\cite{Sicho2020}; the natural-product-likeness score, used wherever African-chemotype character enters a composite, follows the Ertl fragmentation scheme calibrated on natural-product and flat synthetic reference collections\cite{Ertl2008}. The ADMET composite is a weighted mean of predicted absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity endpoints from a single prediction service and is used only for ranking, never as a substitute for experimental pharmacokinetics. Because the composite terms are heterogeneous in origin and uncertainty, the multi-parameter score is treated as a prioritisation device whose components are reported individually wherever a claim depends on one of them.

\subsection{Target selection and structural preparation}\label{sec:target_selection}

Four protein targets are selected to span mechanistically distinct and resistance-relevant vulnerabilities rather than to provide four equivalent binding sites. Dihydrofolate reductase (\emph{Pf}DHFR, PDB 7F3Y, methotrexate copy A702) anchors the antifolate axis and a canonical resistance pathway. The chloroquine resistance transporter (\emph{Pf}CRT) is derived from the 6UKJ cryo-electron microscopy structure of the 7G8 isoform and is corrected to a 3D7-like wild-type receptor by restoring lysine at position \num{76}, since the deposited structure carries the K76T chloroquine-resistance substitution at that position. The caseinolytic protease \emph{Pf}ClpP (PDB 2F6I) represents parasite proteostasis through its chain-A Ser252/His223/Asp219 catalytic triad, and the P-type ATPase \emph{Pf}ATP4 (PDB 9N10) represents ion homeostasis through the D451 phosphorylation-loop region and the DPPR hinge. The paralog structure 4GM2 is not used for \emph{Pf}ClpP interpretation.

Structures are prepared by removing crystallographic waters, assigning protonation states consistent with physiological pH, and adding hydrogen atoms. Binding-site definition combines crystallographic ligand positions with computational pocket detection. The corrected \emph{Pf}CRT receptor was validated independently with P2Rank, whose top predicted pocket scored \num{162.7} at a probability of \num{0.999} with its centre \qty{5.7}{\ang} from the docking grid centre, confirming that the restored wild-type cavity is the pocket targeted by the grid. Grid boxes are target-specific: \qty{25}{\ang} edge length for \emph{Pf}DHFR and \emph{Pf}CRT, \qty{20}{\ang} for \emph{Pf}ClpP, and \qty{24}{\ang} for \emph{Pf}ATP4. \Cref{tab:met_grids} summarises the docking-stage specification per target. The structural evidence behind the four entries is deliberately heterogeneous. \emph{Pf}DHFR carries a co-crystallized inhibitor; the \emph{Pf}CRT site is a cavity proxy rather than a validated small-molecule pocket; and the \emph{Pf}ClpP and \emph{Pf}ATP4 regions are mechanism-motivated because no small-molecule inhibitor is co-crystallized. The panel is therefore treated as a gradient of structural confidence throughout the analysis.

The correction of the \emph{Pf}CRT receptor is described in detail because it is the largest single structural intervention in the panel and because every downstream \emph{Pf}CRT number depends on it. The deposited 6UKJ structure resolves the 7G8 isoform, which carries the K76T substitution associated with chloroquine resistance; docking against the deposited receptor would therefore score chloroquine-like chemotypes against a resistance-state transporter while reporting them as wild-type comparisons. The correction restores lysine at position \num{76} by side-chain replacement on the frozen backbone, and the restored receptor is audited by an independent pocket-detection method rather than assumed correct: the top predicted pocket must localise to the docking box, which it does with a high score and near-coincident centre. The corrected receptor is then used uniformly, so that all wild-type and mutant \emph{Pf}CRT comparisons are computed against the same restored reference. The intervention is recorded as a declared deviation from the deposited structure in the provenance manifest, with the deposited entry retained for reproducibility, and the alternative of using the uncorrected structure is not analysed, because a resistance-state receptor in the wild-type position would contaminate the retention ratios that the analysis exists to compute.

\section{Molecular Docking Protocols}\label{sec:docking_methods}

\subsection{AutoDock Vina docking}\label{sec:vina_docking}

Molecular docking employs AutoDock Vina 1.2.7, a widely validated docking engine that balances computational efficiency with predictive accuracy. The Vina scoring function approximates the binding free energy through a knowledge-based combination of steric, electrostatic, and hydrophobic terms. Ligands are standardised with Open Babel and converted to the PDBQT format with Meeko before docking. Docking is run against fixed target-specific boxes centred on the coordinates described in \Cref{sec:target_selection}, with the exhaustiveness parameter set to \num{64} for all reported scores. Each run returns poses ranked by predicted binding affinity, and the top-ranked pose is retained for downstream analysis.

Docking score interpretation requires careful consideration of the scoring function's limitations. Vina scores are reported in kilocalories per mole and represent an approximation of the binding free energy under idealized conditions. They do not correspond to experimentally measured IC$_{50}$ or $K_{d}$ values, because the scoring function carries systematic biases, neglects entropy contributions, and treats the receptor as a static object. Docking scores are therefore used for relative ranking within a target and never as quantitative predictions of affinity, and no cross-target arithmetic mean is computed because Vina scores are not calibrated across heterogeneous pocket architectures. Every pose is additionally subjected to an automated geometric gate that requires the rank-1 centroid to lie inside the box, at least one ligand atom within \qty{10}{\ang} of the target anchor, and at least \qty{90}{\percent} of ligand atoms inside the padded box. Passing the gate is a pose-quality criterion, not evidence of binding.

\subsection{Docking validation and benchmarking}

Validation of the docking protocol proceeds through three complementary assessments: redocking to the native ligand pose, external benchmarking against the DEKOIS 2.0 dataset, and positive-control enrichment using known actives from the MMV collection. Redocking evaluates the ability of the protocol to reproduce experimentally determined binding modes: for each target with an alignable co-crystallized ligand, the ligand is extracted, redocked into the prepared receptor, and the heavy-atom root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the docked and crystallographic poses is computed, with \qty{2.0}{\ang} used as the success threshold. Redocking reproduced the crystal pose for four of the five alignable ligands (\qty{80}{\percent} success), with at least one successful reproduction for each of the three targets used in the primary analysis. The single failure was the \emph{Pf}DHFR reference ligand methotrexate, whose RMSD was approximately \qty{30}{\ang} because the \emph{Pf}DHFR grid is centred on the NADPH-adjacent site rather than the catalytic methotrexate pocket. This failure is retained in the record rather than repaired, and it illustrates that enrichment outcomes can reflect grid positioning as well as scoring-function behaviour.

The DEKOIS 2.0 benchmark pairs each active compound with property-matched decoys that are topologically distinct, so that enrichment performance reflects molecular recognition rather than trivial separation on molecular weight or lipophilicity. The \emph{Pf}DHFR set comprises \num{40} actives and \num{1200} decoys docked with the same protocol applied to the hybrid library, and performance is summarised by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and the enrichment factor at \num{5} percent ($\mathrm{EF}_{5\%}$). Because the near-chance outcome of this benchmark conditions the interpretation of every absolute Vina score in the docking campaign, a controlled two-arm comparison was added on the identical DEKOIS panel: the deposited receptor with the retained methotrexate was compared with the same receptor after methotrexate removal, using the same grid, frame and exhaustiveness.

Positive-control enrichment uses the MMV Malaria Box under the dual-filter consensus protocol combining AutoDock Vina with DiffDock. A subset of experimentally confirmed antimalarials is docked against the three targets with docking data, and the ROC-AUC of the ranked predictions measures retrodictive consistency with known activity. Enrichment labels for \emph{Pf}CRT and \emph{Pf}ATP4 are score-stratified (top and bottom \num{25} percent) and therefore measure internal consistency rather than independent experimental discrimination. The complete validation results are reported alongside the corresponding docking campaign in Chapter 3.

The consensus arm of this protocol deserves its own description, because it is a distinct estimator rather than a refinement of the Vina score. DiffDock treats docking as a generative task: a diffusion model over the rotation, translation and torsion-angle space of the ligand produces sampled poses, and the model's confidence output replaces the physical scoring function as the ranking signal\cite{Corso2023}. The dual filter retains a candidate only when both estimators place it favourably, which trades recall for precision: a physically parameterised score and a learned generative confidence must agree before a compound advances. The consequence for interpretation is that consensus rankings inherit the failure modes of neither tool alone but also the calibration of neither alone, so consensus output is used for prioritisation and never quoted as an affinity estimate.

Multi-seed reproducibility is quantified for the docking protocol itself. Selected candidate--receptor pairs are redocked under five random seeds with identical ligand preparation and grid boxes, and the dispersion of the lowest-energy modes is reported as the seed sensitivity of the protocol. This bound is protocol-local: it characterises the stochasticity of the search algorithm under fixed inputs and says nothing about pose accuracy, force-field adequacy or binding. It is nevertheless the quantity that any ratio-based score computed from docking outputs inherits, because numerator and denominator fluctuate under the same seed distribution. A bounded score-perturbation audit completes the robustness picture: the resistance-score classes are recomputed after adding deterministic perturbations of up to $\pm\qty{1}{\kcalmol}$ to every score, and the fraction of class assignments that survives the perturbation is reported with the results.

\subsection{Independent and CNN-based cross-scoring}

Two further cross-checks extend the validation of the docking protocol beyond the primary campaign. The first is a secondary cross-docking calibration on the QuickVina engine, a Vina derivative, applied to the primary-lead panel against three independent receptor structures; the resulting composite MPO--score association is null (Spearman $\rho = \num{0.0129}$, $p = \num{0.0913}$), which bounds any claim that the multi-parameter optimization and the docking scores measure the same quantity.

The second is a deep-learning-based rescoring of a \num{39}-compound sensitivity panel across the same eight \emph{Pf}DHFR and \emph{Pf}CRT states as the primary cohort, producing \num{312} finite docking complexes scored by both AutoDock Vina and the GNINA convolutional-neural-network affinity model. The panel serves two declared purposes: it measures the sensitivity of the ratio-based score to a second scoring function (bootstrap mean docking RRS \num{100.45}, \numrange{99.59}{101.32}; Class-A fraction \num{0.974}, \numrange{0.923}{1.000}), and it tests whether class assignments survive independent rescoring (\qty{100}{\percent} class-level agreement with Vina, with no per-mutant rank transfer claimed; per-mutant rank correlations are near zero for C59R and I164L). Because almost the whole panel falls in Class~A, the agreement is close to degenerate and constrains gross class assignment only. This is a computational sensitivity check, not validation of the candidate ranking or of experimental resistance.

\subsection{Retrospective genotype--phenotype confrontation}

The static docking estimand is additionally confronted, retrospectively and outside the candidate cohort, with published genotype-resolved inhibition data. Inhibition records for \emph{Pf}DHFR (ChEMBL target CHEMBL1939) are curated by assay description, which encodes the enzyme genotype, yielding \num{28} compounds with paired wild-type and quadruple-mutant (N51I/C59R/S108N/I164L) IC$_{50}$ measurements, \num{24} of which also carry paired double-mutant (C59R/S108N) values, and triple-mutant (C59R/S108N/I164L) pairs complete for all \num{28}. The compounds are docked against wild-type, double, triple and quadruple \emph{Pf}DHFR receptors under the declared protocol of \Cref{sec:vina_docking} (exhaustiveness \num{64}, fixed \emph{Pf}DHFR box, single seed \num{42}; \num{112}/\num{112} jobs completed); mutant receptors are generated from the wild-type coordinates by in-silico side-chain replacement with backbone coordinates unchanged, then prepared identically. For each genotype the score difference $\Delta\Delta G = s_{\mathrm{mutant}} - s_{\mathrm{WT}}$ is compared with the published fold-shift $\log_2(\mathrm{IC}_{50,\mathrm{mut}}/\mathrm{IC}_{50,\mathrm{WT}})$ by Spearman rank correlation, direction agreement, and an exact binomial probability of the observed departure from chance agreement.

The comparison carries two structural limitations that are declared with the protocol. The reference IC$_{50}$ values are whole-parasite growth measurements in DHFR-genotyped but non-isogenic strains, so they form a phenotype-level reference rather than a binding-affinity ground truth, and permeability, efflux and background mutations all contribute to the endpoint; and the curated compounds are disjoint from the candidate cohort, so the result audits the estimand itself rather than validating or invalidating any candidate class. Assays identifiers, raw records and provenance are frozen with the analysis.

\subsection{Candidate selection cascade}

The transition from the expanded library to the resistance-analysis cohort is a filter cascade whose every stage is declared, because each stage changes the population that later statistics describe. Starting from the \num{19913} molecules that survive synthetic-accessibility and multi-parameter filtering, the cascade applies, in order: a multi-parameter optimization threshold (score at least \num{0.70}, retaining the primary leads), a predicted synthetic-accessibility requirement (positive SYBA score), a predicted selectivity requirement (selectivity index above \num{10}), a target-coverage requirement (docking evidence against at least two of the four receptors), and a scaffold-diversity cap (at most three compounds per Bemis--Murcko scaffold). The cascade yields the seventeen-member Set-C cohort used throughout the resistance analyses. Every filter is a computational prediction, so the cohort is a chain of conditional selections and its composition is conditional on all of them; class frequencies within the cohort are reported with this conditioning attached and are never extrapolated to the parent library. The cascade also fixes which comparisons are legitimate: candidates excluded at different stages or evaluated against different target subsets are not pooled into a single statistic without the coverage difference being declared, which is why the two-target primary set and the single-target remainder are always reported separately.

\section{Resistance-Resilient Scoring and Polypharmacology}\label{sec:rrs_methods}

\subsection{Estimand architecture of the resistance analysis}

The resistance analysis distinguishes, by construction, four layers of analysis that answer different questions and are never merged. The primary layer is the docking-score-retention analysis: for the candidates with eligible wild-type docking scores on both the dihydrofolate-reductase and chloroquine-resistance-transporter panels, ratio-based retention classes are computed under the declared protocol. The second layer is the static-to-dynamic divergence comparison, which asks whether the primary retention metric and a trajectory-based geometric retention ratio point in the same direction on the matched systems for which both exist; this layer is a comparison of estimands, not a validation of either. The third layer comprises secondary structural-retention diagnostics, including the non-overlapping parent-study wild-type complexes and the single-ligand feasibility probe, which address pose retention and endpoint feasibility on different coverage and sampling questions and are therefore reported separately from the primary classes. The fourth layer is technical validation: protocol reproducibility, seed sensitivity, rescoring by an independent scoring function, and bounded perturbation audits. Each layer carries its own scope statement, and no quantity from one layer is presented as evidence within another. This architecture exists because the most damaging interpretive error in this class of study is not a wrong number but a right number quoted under the wrong estimand: a protocol-local retention class, a geometric pose-retention ratio, and a phenotype-level resistance measurement are three different quantities, and the analysis never allows one to substitute silently for another.

The reference controls for the docking and dynamics layers are five approved antimalarial drugs spanning distinct mechanisms of action: artemisinin, pyrimethamine, chloroquine, lumefantrine and cipargamin. They serve as calibration anchors for the docking protocol and, where trajectories exist, as familiar behavioural reference points for the dynamics; they are not part of the candidate cohort and contribute no retention classes.

\subsection{Resistance-resilience score computation}\label{sec:rrs_definition}

Resistance-resilient scoring (RRS) quantifies a compound's predicted ability to retain binding across clinically relevant resistance mutations. The score is defined per target and per mutation from the ratio of Vina score magnitudes rather than from a difference of free energies, because absolute Vina values are not comparable across receptor states. For candidate $i$, mutation $m$ and target $t$, with $S_{\mathrm{Vina}}$ denoting the signed Vina output,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RRS}_{i,m,t} = 100 \times \frac{\left| S_{\mathrm{Vina},i,m,t} \right|}{\left| S_{\mathrm{Vina},i,\mathrm{WT},t} \right|}.
\label{eq:rrs}
\end{equation}

Candidates whose wild-type score magnitude falls below \qty{5.0}{\kcalmol} are excluded from that target's score-retention analysis. This refers to the separate wild-type/mutant panel for the target in question, not to the four-target wild-type matrix, and non-binding targets are excluded rather than assigned a zero score or used as a denominator. The available-target summary averages the defined target-specific values across eligible targets and mutation states. RRS is a relative docking-score-retention statistic: it is not a free-energy difference and not a measurement of resistance.

Classes are assigned from retention thresholds and are mutually exclusive. Class A* requires all available mutant RRS values to be at least \qty{80}{\percent} and the minimum eligible wild-type score magnitude to be at least \qty{7.0}{\kcalmol}. Class A requires all available values to be at least \qty{80}{\percent} without the wild-type anchor criterion. Class B requires all available values to be at least \qty{70}{\percent} with at least one below \qty{80}{\percent}. Class C requires at least one value to be at least \qty{80}{\percent} without meeting the A, A* or B criteria, and Class D is assigned when no available mutant RRS value reaches \qty{80}{\percent}. The cut-offs follow the Vina score distribution in this panel and are not calibrated to biochemical resistance or clinical outcome, so sensitivity to alternative thresholds is reported as part of the resistance-scoring analysis. \Cref{tab:met_rrs_classes} states the class definitions in one place, because the mutually exclusive partition is easier to audit as a table than as running text.

For \emph{Pf}DHFR, four resistance-associated substitutions are modelled: N51I, C59R, S108N and I164L. For \emph{Pf}CRT, two states are modelled: K76T, which is the chloroquine-resistance marker, and K76A, which serves as a mechanistic comparator for perturbation of Lys76 and carries no clinical prevalence claim. No mutant crystal structures were available, so homology models were generated with SWISS-MODEL from the wild-type templates 7F3Y and 6UKJ\cite{Waterhouse2018}. A model was retained only when the QMEAN score exceeded \num{-4.0}, the GMQE exceeded \num{0.7}, Ramachandran-favoured residues exceeded \qty{95}{\percent}, and the backbone RMSD relative to the template was below \qty{1.5}{\ang}; all six models met these thresholds. Model scores are not experimental structures, and this constraint is carried into the interpretation of every mutant comparison.

The eligibility rule that excludes weak wild-type denominators is stated as part of the score definition rather than as a post-processing choice, because it changes the estimand. A ratio of score magnitudes is unstable when its denominator approaches zero: a wild-type score of \qty{-1}{\kcalmol} against a mutant score of \qty{-4}{\kcalmol} would produce a retention ratio of \num{400}\,\% that reflects score noise rather than mutation response. Excluding targets whose wild-type magnitude falls below \qty{5.0}{\kcalmol} removes this failure mode at the cost of coverage, since candidates without an eligible denominator on a target are reported as single-target rather than dropped silently. The eligibility threshold itself was subjected to a bounded sensitivity check (moving it from \qty{4.0}{\kcalmol} to \qty{5.0}{\kcalmol}), and the class assignment is invariant under that move. This property is reported with the results rather than assumed, because invariance under one alternative threshold does not establish invariance under all of them.

\subsection{Polypharmacology network similarity}

The resistance-aware workflow does not compute a cross-target arithmetic composite, because Vina scores are not calibrated across different pocket architectures. Target breadth is read instead from the pattern of target-specific evidence, using a within-target favourability criterion: a target counts as favourable for a candidate when the candidate's Vina estimate falls at or below that target's median across the cohort. On the \num{17}-member cohort the per-target medians were \num{-5.92} kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ for \emph{Pf}DHFR, \num{-8.06} kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ for \emph{Pf}CRT, \num{-6.02} kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ for \emph{Pf}ClpP and \num{-6.38} kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ for \emph{Pf}ATP4. This within-target definition avoids a single cross-target cutoff and is a prioritisation device rather than an affinity threshold.

Alongside this per-target profile, a polypharmacology network similarity (PNS) is computed as a network-weighted ranking score from a STRING protein--protein interaction network at a confidence threshold of \num{700}\cite{Szklarczyk2023}. The score formalises the network layer as follows. The association network assigns every protein pair a confidence weight, and a candidate's target set induces a subnetwork of the proteins it engages; the candidate's proximity to the antimalarial reference targets is computed as a weighted combination of shortest-path distances through the association graph, in which each edge is discounted by the confidence weight of the association so that speculative links contribute less than experimentally supported ones. Formally, for candidate $i$ with target set $T_i$, reference target set $R$, and network distance $d_{\mathrm{net}}(u, v)$ defined as the minimum over paths of the sum of inverse-confidence edge costs,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PNS}_i = \frac{1}{|R|} \sum_{r \in R} \min_{u \in T_i} \; d_{\mathrm{net}}(u, r),
\label{eq:pns}
\end{equation}

so that a candidate engaging a protein directly linked to a reference target receives a lower, more favourable value than one whose nearest connection is indirect. The score measures how closely a candidate's target profile sits to established antimalarial network hubs. \emph{Pf}CRT is absent from the queried network, so its centrality is imputed as the network mean (\num{0.151}) and the sensitivity of the ranking to that imputation is reported explicitly. Two reference scores are computed for comparison: a network position that tracks established targets, and a measure of chemical-space position described in the next subsection. Associations between RRS, PNS and this chemical-space descriptor are evaluated with Spearman rank correlation, with permutation testing over \num{100000} permutations, Bonferroni adjustment for three tests, and percentile bootstrap intervals from \num{10000} resamples. At the cohort sizes involved these correlations are descriptive rather than powered tests of independence.

The network layer carries declared robustness checks of two kinds. The first is imputation sensitivity: the absent \emph{Pf}CRT centrality is replaced not only by the network mean but also by zero, one-half and twice the reference value, and the rank correlation of the resulting PNS ordering against the reference ordering is reported, so that the reader can see how much of the ranking depends on a value that no data source provides. The second is network-construction sensitivity: the STRING confidence cutoff itself (\num{400}, \num{700} and \num{900}) is varied, and the stability of the ranking across the three networks is quantified by pairwise rank correlations. Both checks bound the same quantity from different sides: the first asks how the score responds to a missing input, the second asks how it responds to an arbitrary threshold on a complete one. Neither check speaks to biological target importance, and neither is presented as doing so.

\subsection{African-chemotype structural index}

The African-chemotype structural index (ACSI) places a candidate relative to approved-drug and African natural-product reference spaces. It is a weighted composite of four min--max normalised components,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ACSI} = 0.40\, D_{\mathrm{DrugBank}} + 0.25\, D_{\mathrm{ANPDB}} + 0.20\, f_{sp^3} + 0.15\, \mathrm{NPL},
\label{eq:acsi}
\end{equation}

where $D_{\mathrm{DrugBank}}$ and $D_{\mathrm{ANPDB}}$ are the minimum Tanimoto distances to the approved-drug and African natural-product reference sets respectively, $f_{sp^3}$ is the fraction of $sp^3$-hybridized carbons, and NPL is a natural-product-likeness score. All four components are normalised over the cohort under analysis, so ACSI is a relative rather than an absolute coordinate and its weights carry no mechanistic claim; weight-sensitivity checks are reported with the resistance-scoring results. ACSI describes chemical-space position and is not used as a substitute for target-specific mutation analysis.

\section{Molecular Dynamics Simulation Methods}\label{sec:md_methods}

\subsection{System preparation and parameterization}

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide time-resolved, atomistic descriptions of drug--target interactions and allow the geometric and energetic consequences of a docking pose to be examined. All simulations are run with GROMACS\cite{Abraham2015}, with proteins parameterised under the CHARMM36m force field\cite{Huang2017CHARMM36m}. System preparation begins from the selected docking pose for each compound--target pair. The protein is parameterised with CHARMM36m, which has been extensively validated for proteins. Two ligand parameterisation protocols were used and are reported separately. For the four parent-study wild-type complexes, ligands were parameterised with GAFF2. For the \num{16}-system Set-C pilot, ligands were parameterised with the OpenFF 2.2.0 force field using AM1-BCC partial charges. The substitution of OpenFF for GAFF2 in the Set-C pilot, and its earlier substitution for the originally planned CGF force field, are recorded in the provenance manifests as declared, approved policy deviations; because the two cohorts use different ligand force fields, their absolute MM-GBSA values are never compared with one another.

The protein--ligand complex is placed in a rectangular simulation box with periodic boundary conditions, ensuring a minimum distance of \qty{12}{\ang} between the solute and the box edges, and solvated with the TIP3P water model. Counterions are added to neutralise the system and to reach a physiological ionic strength of \num{0.15} M. Energy minimisation proceeds in two stages: steepest descent with heavy-atom restraints, followed by unrestrained conjugate-gradient minimisation. Convergence is assessed by monitoring the maximum force against a fixed threshold.

\subsection{Equilibration and production protocols}

Equilibration proceeds through a three-stage protocol designed to relax the system gradually while avoiding structural artifacts. The first stage runs under the NVT ensemble at \qty{310.15}{\kelvin} with position restraints on heavy atoms to allow solvent equilibration, using a velocity-rescaling thermostat. The second stage runs under the NPT ensemble at the same temperature and \num{1.0} bar with reduced position restraints to equilibrate the system density, using a Parrinello--Rahman barostat. The third stage continues under NPT without restraints to reach the final equilibrated state. Equilibration quality is assessed by monitoring temperature, pressure, density, potential energy, and the RMSD of protein backbone atoms; a system is considered equilibrated when these properties fluctuate stably around their mean values for a sustained interval. \Cref{tab:met_md_stages} summarises the ensemble, thermostat, barostat and restraint schedule per stage.

\subsection{Trajectory analysis and binding free energy estimation}

Trajectory analysis employs standard GROMACS utilities together with MDAnalysis\cite{MichaudAgrawal2011} for Python-based workflows. Structural stability is assessed through the RMSD of protein backbone atoms and ligand heavy atoms relative to their initial conformations, and per-residue flexibility is assessed through the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF). RMSD is computed as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSD}(t) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} \left| \mathbf{r}_{i}(t) - \mathbf{r}_{i}(\mathrm{ref}) \right|^{2} },
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the number of atoms, $\mathbf{r}_{i}(t)$ is the position of atom $i$ at time $t$, and $\mathbf{r}_{i}(\mathrm{ref})$ is the reference position; RMSF is computed as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSF}(i) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t} \left| \mathbf{r}_{i}(t) - \langle \mathbf{r}_{i} \rangle \right|^{2} },
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the number of frames and $\langle \mathbf{r}_{i} \rangle$ is the time-averaged position of residue $i$. Contacts, hydrogen bonds and the radius of gyration are computed on a decimated subset of frames, while RMSD is evaluated on every frame after periodic-boundary discontinuities are removed. Ligand retention is summarised by the minimum protein--ligand heavy-atom distance computed per frame, from which a bound fraction is derived. Ligand--protein interaction fingerprints are computed over stored snapshots to measure the persistence of individual contacts.

Binding free energy estimation uses the molecular mechanics/generalised Born surface area (MM-GBSA) approach implemented in \texttt{gmx\_MMPBSA}\cite{ValdesTresanco2021}, with the GB$^{\mathrm{OBC2}}$ model and a salt concentration of \num{0.15} M. The endpoint is computed as

\begin{equation}
\Delta G_{\mathrm{bind}} = \langle G_{\mathrm{complex}} \rangle - \langle G_{\mathrm{protein}} \rangle - \langle G_{\mathrm{ligand}} \rangle,
\end{equation}

where each term is decomposed as

\begin{equation}
G = E_{\mathrm{MM}} + G_{\mathrm{solv}} - TS,
\end{equation}

with $E_{\mathrm{MM}}$ the molecular mechanics energy, $G_{\mathrm{solv}}$ the solvation free energy, and $TS$ the entropic contribution. Beyond the total endpoint, the per-term decomposition ($E_{\mathrm{vdW}}$, $E_{\mathrm{elec}}$, polar and non-polar solvation) and the per-residue contribution to binding are computed from the same snapshots. The per-residue decomposition serves a diagnostic purpose: it identifies which pocket residues dominate the enthalpic endpoint, which can be compared with the interaction-fingerprint anchors as a consistency check between the energetic and the geometric view. It is not used for lead optimisation, because per-residue endpoint contributions at this sampling carry the same within-protocol scope as the total endpoint. Configurational entropy is not included in the reported endpoints, and the MMPBSA conversion from CHARMM36 to AMBER parameters is documented as part of the protocol. Endpoints are reported with the frame-level standard error for the interpretable complexes and with the propagated within-trajectory standard deviation for the Set-C table; neither quantity is a replicate-level uncertainty estimate. Endpoint energies at this trajectory length are within-protocol contrasts and are not used to rank dissimilar compounds.

Where independent wild-type replicates exist, their endpoint difference defines the empirical noise floor of the endpoint estimator: the magnitude of the difference between two nominally identical systems bounds the variation that the protocol itself produces without any mutation. This floor is reported with the results and used as the reference scale against which an inter-replicate sensitivity is judged material. It is an estimator-scale quantity, not a physical energy: it characterises the protocol, not the binding.

Trajectory quality control comprises the absence of fatal errors in the GROMACS log files, the absence of LINCS warnings indicating unstable bond constraints, the absence of not-a-number values in energy or coordinate files, and the absence of gaps in the trajectory files. A geometric contact-stability criterion is applied in addition: a trajectory enters endpoint analysis only if the minimum protein--ligand heavy-atom distance remains below \qty{3.5}{\ang}. Systems failing any check are flagged, and the failure is reported rather than corrected.

The quality-control contract is deliberately asymmetric between acceptance and interpretation. Acceptance is binary: a trajectory either satisfies every check or it is excluded from the corresponding analysis class, and the excluded record keeps its identifier and failure mode in the output ledger. Interpretation is scope-bound: a passing trajectory supports statements about pose retention and local geometry at the simulated timescale, and nothing beyond that. The contract also specifies what is not checked. No convergence test on free-energy estimates is applied, because endpoint values at this trajectory length are not converged quantities and pretending otherwise would manufacture a precision that the sampling cannot support; no equivalence bound between replicates is assumed, because replicate variance is itself an output of the analysis where replicates exist rather than a known constant; and no visual inspection enters the acceptance decision, so that the contract is auditable from the recorded files alone.

Interaction-fingerprint analysis provides the contact-level complement to these global geometric summaries. The ProLIF library converts each stored snapshot into a binary interaction fingerprint whose bits encode hydrophobic contacts, $\pi$--$\pi$ stacking, hydrogen bonds and cation--$\pi$ interactions between specific protein--ligand atom pairs\cite{Bouysset2021}. Averaging the fingerprints over snapshots yields a per-residue occupancy between \num{0} and \num{1} for each interaction type, which is the quantity reported as contact persistence. Occupancy answers a different question from the minimum-distance or endpoint-energy summaries: it identifies which specific residues anchor the ligand and how consistently each anchor persists, whereas the geometric summaries measure whether the ligand stays in the pocket as a whole. The two views can disagree, and the disagreement is informative, because a ligand can retain a bounded position while exchanging one anchor for another, a pattern that distance statistics smooth over and occupancy statistics expose.

Beyond endpoint energies, trajectory-based retention is summarised by a distance ratio defined on the matched Set-C systems,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MD\text{-}RRS}_{d} = 100 \times \frac{\langle d_{\min} \rangle_{\mathrm{mut}}}{\langle d_{\min} \rangle_{\mathrm{WT}}},
\label{eq:mdrrs}
\end{equation}

where $\langle d_{\min} \rangle$ is the mean minimum protein--ligand heavy-atom distance over the trajectory of the mutant or wild-type system. Values at or below \qty{100}{\percent} indicate that the mutant pose is retained at least as tightly as the wild-type endpoint. The quantity is deliberately named as a distance ratio and never as an affinity: it is a geometric retention statistic of the same ratio form as the docking RRS of \Cref{sec:rrs_definition} but estimated from an entirely different procedure, which is precisely what makes the two quantities non-interchangeable estimands in the analysis of Chapter 3.

The comparison that the ratio enables is directional by design, and the direction rule is fixed before the trajectories are analysed. For each matched mutant state with an eligible wild-type docking baseline, the comparison records whether the static retention ratio falls below \num{100}\,\%, indicating that the docking scores penalise the mutant, while the distance ratio lies at or below \num{100}\,\%, indicating that the trajectory retains the pose at least as tightly as the wild type. The fraction of matched states exhibiting this disagreement is reported with an interval that accounts for the small number of matched comparisons, so that the reader can see both the observed direction and the sampling limit on it. Three interpretive boundaries are attached. The comparison requires no assumption that either quantity estimates affinity, only that each is internally consistent under its own protocol; the matched-state set is fixed by eligibility rather than chosen after inspection; and the fraction is a property of this panel under this protocol, so it is a calibration observation rather than a general docking-failure rate.

\section{Classical and Quantum-Inspired Molecular Descriptors}\label{sec:descriptors_methods}

\subsection{Extended-connectivity fingerprints}

Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP), also known as Morgan or circular fingerprints, encode the local chemical environment of each atom within a specified radius. The algorithm proceeds iteratively: at iteration zero each atom is assigned an initial identifier derived from its atomic properties, and at each subsequent iteration the identifier is updated by hashing the current identifier together with the identifiers of neighbouring atoms. After a fixed number of iterations the set of all identifiers encountered constitutes the fingerprint. ECFP4 (radius \num{2}) is the standard choice, balancing structural resolution against computational efficiency.

ECFP4 is computed with RDKit 2025.03.6 as a \num{2048}-bit Morgan fingerprint with radius \num{2}. Fingerprint similarity between two molecules is quantified by the Tanimoto coefficient,

\begin{equation}
T(A, B) = \frac{\left| A \cap B \right|}{\left| A \cup B \right|},
\end{equation}

where $A$ and $B$ are the sets of substructures encoded in the fingerprints. Values range from \num{0} (no shared substructures) to \num{1} (identical fingerprints). Across the representation analyses the following classical fingerprints are computed as baselines with the RDKit toolkit\cite{LandrumRDKit}: ECFP4, MACCS \num{167}-bit structural keys, atom-pair, binding-pattern, functional-class ECFP4, 2D-pharmacophore, and Morgan atom-pair counts. The seven descriptors span three distinct encoding principles, and the spread is deliberate. Hashed circular fingerprints (ECFP4 and its functional-class variant) encode local atom environments without a dictionary; keyed structural benchmarks (MACCS) and pharmacophore fingerprints map onto predefined dictionaries of substructures or feature triples; and the atom-pair family encodes pairs of atoms at a given topological distance rather than connected neighbourhoods. Because a representation benchmark can only be interpreted relative to the family its baseline belongs to, the primary baseline is ECFP4, and the remaining six serve as family representatives whose relative ordering is reported descriptively. ECFP4 serves as the primary baseline against which every quantum-inspired and learned representation is evaluated.

\subsection{Topological fingerprints via persistent homology}\label{sec:tfp_methods}

Topological data analysis extracts global structural features from molecular graphs through persistent homology, which tracks the birth and death of topological features as a filtration parameter increases. For a molecule, a three-dimensional conformer is generated with the RDKit ETKDG distance-geometry generator\cite{Riniker2015} at a fixed random seed, and a weighted undirected graph is constructed with atoms as vertices and edge weights

\begin{equation}
w_{ij} = d_{ij} \left( 1 + \frac{Z_i Z_j}{100} \right),
\end{equation}

where $d_{ij}$ is the Euclidean distance between atoms $i$ and $j$ and $Z_i$, $Z_j$ are their atomic numbers, so that interactions involving heavier atoms are weighted more strongly. Vietoris--Rips persistence diagrams are computed up to homology dimension two using Ripser.py. Persistence in dimension zero describes connected components, dimension one describes cycles, and dimension two describes voids, so the representation captures ring topology and cavity structure that local substructure hashing does not resolve.

Each diagram is vectorised into scalar summaries per homology dimension, including the persistence entropy

\begin{equation}
H_d^{\mathrm{ent}} = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i, \qquad p_i = \frac{\mathrm{pers}_i}{\sum_j \mathrm{pers}_j},
\end{equation}

the count of features whose persistence exceeds a fixed threshold, the maximum persistence and the mean persistence. The primary activity analysis uses a \num{78}-feature representation obtained by combining these homology summaries with persistence-image and Betti-curve components, while the \num{12}-feature core is evaluated separately as a descriptive topological comparison. Because the descriptors are derived from three-dimensional conformers, they encode geometric persistence rather than graph-theoretic indices.

\subsection{Tensor network embeddings}\label{sec:tne_methods}

Tensor networks compress high-dimensional data by exploiting low-rank structure, and provide a fixed-length, physically interpretable embedding of a molecule. For each molecule a three-dimensional tensor $T \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F \times 3}$ is constructed with $N = \num{100}$ maximum atoms (zero-padded), $F = \num{10}$ per-atom features (atomic number, degree, total hydrogens, formal charge, aromaticity, hybridization, mass, electronegativity, bond count and ring membership), and the three Cartesian coordinates, so that $T_{i,f,c} = x_{i,f} \, r_{i,c}$.

Each tensor is compressed by Tucker decomposition with mode ranks $(d, d, 3)$ at bond dimension $d = \num{8}$,

\begin{equation}
T \approx \mathcal{G} \times_1 U^{(1)} \times_2 U^{(2)} \times_3 U^{(3)},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{G} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d \times 3}$ is the core tensor, and the descriptor is the flattened core of $d^{2} \times 3 = \num{192}$ elements. For a molecule with $N$ actual atoms the real compression ratio is $\mathrm{CR}_{\mathrm{real}}(N) = 10N / d^{2}$, which at the mean panel size of approximately \num{39} atoms corresponds to roughly \num{6.1}-fold compression relative to the real-atom representation. Compression quality is quantified by the relative Frobenius reconstruction error. Because mode rank and bond dimension are the free parameters of the representation, the descriptor is evaluated at several settings in the dedicated sensitivity analysis.

Every three-dimensional descriptor in this thesis inherits a conformer-generation stage, and the stage is governed rather than left implicit. A single low-energy conformer per molecule is generated with fixed random seed and standard distance-geometry bounds, without subsequent geometry optimisation, so that the mapping from graph to geometry is deterministic given the software version. This choice has a declared cost: conformational flexibility is collapsed to one representative geometry, so descriptors that read ring topology and connectivity are robust to the choice while descriptors sensitive to substituent orientation are not. Where a descriptor family consumes three-dimensional input, the same conformer set is shared across all consumers in a given analysis, so that differences between descriptors reflect their encoding rather than different geometry samples. The dependency is recorded in the provenance manifest together with the generator version and seed, because a conformer regeneration with a different seed or generator can shift a descriptor value without any change to the molecular graph, which would otherwise look like an unexplained reproducibility failure.

\subsection{Quantum kernel similarity}

Quantum kernel methods embed molecular representations into a Hilbert space defined by a quantum feature map, allowing similarity to be computed from quantum state overlaps. The quantum kernel similarity between molecules $A$ and $B$ is

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{quantum}}(A, B) = \left| \langle \phi(A) \mid \phi(B) \rangle \right|^{2},
\end{equation}

where $\left| \phi(A) \right\rangle$ and $\left| \phi(B) \right\rangle$ are quantum states prepared from the corresponding molecular feature vectors. The feature map is implemented with the IQP embedding template of PennyLane 0.45.1 on a six-qubit statevector simulator, so that the state is prepared as

\begin{equation}
\left| \phi(\mathbf{x}) \right\rangle = U_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}) \left| 0 \right\rangle^{\otimes n},
\end{equation}

where $U_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ applies single-qubit rotations and two-qubit entangling gates whose angles are functions of products of feature values. The input vector for each molecule is the Morgan atom-pair count vector (radius \num{2}, \num{2048} features) reduced to six dimensions by uniform manifold approximation and projection under the Jaccard metric at a fixed random state, then scaled to the interval $[-1, 1]$.

The quantum kernel score (QKS) is the area under the ROC curve obtained by a support vector machine trained on the precomputed quantum kernel matrix. The corresponding classical comparator is a radial basis function kernel,

\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{RBF}}(A, B) = \exp \left( -\gamma \left\| \mathbf{x}_{A} - \mathbf{x}_{B} \right\|^{2} \right),
\end{equation}

whose bandwidth $\gamma$ is selected by inner cross-validation over a fixed candidate grid, and a linear kernel provides a second reference point. Descriptor representations are evaluated with random forests and kernel representations with support vector machines, because a support vector machine accepts a precomputed kernel directly; the two families are therefore never presented as a like-for-like model comparison. All quantum kernel values reported here are computed on a classical statevector simulator. No hardware-level quantum advantage is claimed from this implementation, and evaluation on near-term quantum hardware is deferred to the quantum machine learning programme, whose protocol is established in the part of this thesis that follows.

Because two kernels can be compared at several levels that are easy to conflate, the diagnostic hierarchy is fixed in advance. The first level is kernel--target alignment, which measures how strongly the kernel matrix concentrates on same-label pairs and is reported as a descriptive geometric quantity. The second level is classification performance under cross-validation, which is the quantity that matters for any claim about predictive utility. The hierarchy exists because the two levels can move in opposite directions: a feature map whose induced geometry aligns well with the labels can still yield a classifier indistinguishable from a tuned classical kernel, because alignment is a property of the average geometry while classification is decided by the decision boundary in the region where classes overlap. The alignment statistic is therefore reported alongside performance, never in its place, and the interpretation rule is declared before the results: a difference at the alignment level without a difference at the performance level is recorded as a geometric observation, not as an advantage.

\section{Machine Learning and Deep Learning Architectures}\label{sec:ml_methods}

\subsection{Random forest baselines}

Random forests provide a robust and interpretable baseline for molecular property prediction. A random forest is an ensemble of decision trees in which each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the training data and considers only a random subset of features at each split point, with predictions aggregated across trees by majority voting or averaging. Random forests are well suited to high-dimensional descriptor spaces, resist overfitting through ensemble averaging, and expose feature importance scores that support interpretability, which makes them the appropriate baseline when the object of study is a molecular representation rather than a model class.

Two random-forest configurations appear in this thesis and are reported separately wherever they are compared. For the descriptor benchmark, descriptor representations are evaluated with random forests of \num{200} trees under stratified cross-validation. For the learned-representation benchmark, the ECFP4--RF baseline uses a random forest of \num{500} trees trained on \num{2048}-bit ECFP4 fingerprints. Class imbalance, where present, is addressed through class-weight adjustment rather than resampling, so that the training distribution is not altered. In both benchmarks the baseline configuration is fixed before the comparative experiments are run and is held constant, so that differences between representations are not attributable to baseline tuning.

\subsection{Graph isomorphism networks}

Graph isomorphism networks (GINs) are a class of graph neural networks designed to achieve maximal expressive power within the message-passing framework. The GIN layer updates node representations according to

\begin{equation}
h_{v}^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)} \left( (1 + \varepsilon^{(k)}) \cdot h_{v}^{(k)} + \sum_{u \in N(v)} h_{u}^{(k)} \right),
\end{equation}

where $h_{v}^{(k)}$ is the representation of node $v$ at layer $k$, $N(v)$ denotes the neighbours of $v$, $\mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}$ is a multi-layer perceptron, and $\varepsilon^{(k)}$ is a learnable parameter. This formulation is provably as powerful as the Weisfeiler--Lehman graph isomorphism test, which means that a GIN can distinguish any pair of non-isomorphic graphs that the test can distinguish. It also fixes the expressive ceiling of the architecture: structural motifs that the test cannot resolve, including certain bridged polycyclic cages and macrocyclic systems, are not separated by local message aggregation regardless of depth or width. This bound motivates the fusion architectures evaluated in the learned-representation benchmark.

For molecular property prediction, atoms serve as nodes with initial features encoding atomic number, degree, formal charge, hybridization, aromaticity and ring membership, and bonds serve as edges with features encoding bond type and stereochemistry. The GIN implementation comprises three message-passing layers with \num{128} hidden units, dropout of \num{0.1}, rectified linear activations and batch normalisation after each message-passing layer, followed by global sum pooling. Two fusion variants are evaluated alongside the base architecture. GIN--TFP concatenates the graph embedding with the \num{78}-dimensional persistent-homology vector described in \Cref{sec:tfp_methods}, and GIN--TNE replaces that block with the \num{192}-dimensional tensor network embedding described in \Cref{sec:tne_methods}. Training uses the Adam optimiser with a fixed learning rate and weight decay, and the message-passing configuration is held constant across the fusion variants so that the comparison isolates the contribution of the fused descriptor block. A separate capacity sweep over hidden width and dropout bounds the alternative explanation that any deficit of the graph architecture is a tuning artifact.

\subsection{Transformer-based molecular representations}

Transformer models, originally developed for natural language processing, have been adapted for molecular property prediction by treating SMILES strings as sequences of tokens. ChemBERTa is a transformer pre-trained on a large corpus of unlabeled chemical structures by masked language modelling, so that the model learns contextualised representations of molecular substructures by predicting masked tokens within SMILES strings.

For antimalarial activity prediction, ChemBERTa is fine-tuned with the Hugging Face Transformers library. Canonical SMILES strings are tokenised, the pre-trained model is loaded, and a classification head is placed on top of the pooled sequence representation before fine-tuning on the activity labels. A methodological safeguard is applied specifically to this architecture during cross-validation: pretrained weights are restored from the original checkpoint before each fold, so that no fold's fine-tuned weights seed another fold. Enforcing per-fold weight restoration reduces the risk of cross-fold information transfer that can inflate performance estimates of a sequence model during cross-validation, and it is reported as a reproducibility safeguard rather than as an optimisation. Fine-tuning and evaluation follow the same partition families applied to the fingerprint and graph baselines.

\section{Statistical Analysis and Validation Frameworks}\label{sec:stats_methods}

\subsection{Activity-label provenance and panel freezing}

Every supervised comparison in the representation analyses consumes the same label layer, and its provenance is declared here once. The primary panel is frozen at the molecules with complete descriptor coverage; binary activity labels are produced by a single external prediction model at a fixed decision threshold, giving a cohort in which roughly three quarters of the molecules are labelled active. These labels are model outputs, not assay measurements, so every classification metric in the results chapters measures agreement with one model's opinion of activity, and the phrase \emph{activity prediction} is used in exactly that sense throughout. Three structural consequences follow and are respected by construction. First, the prevalence determines the ceiling of any early-recognition metric, so enrichment-style metrics are reported only together with their prevalence-imposed ceilings. Second, the label model has its own inductive bias, most likely favouring scaffold families over-represented in its training data, so the scaffold-grouped analyses double as a partial control on label-model bias. Third, the transfer analysis uses a separately sourced label set built from experimentally reported inhibition values under a declared potency cutoff, which changes the label semantics between panels; internal and transfer results are therefore never pooled, and the transfer result is interpreted as a label-source-shift assessment rather than as a hold-out validation, because molecule-level disjointness between the panels was not established.

\subsection{Chemical-space characterisation of the candidate cohort}

Before any resistance or representation analysis, the candidate cohort is characterised on a fixed descriptor set so that its chemical-space position is known independently of any performance result. The characterisation reports the mean and dispersion of molecular weight, octanol--water partition coefficient, quantitative drug-likeness estimate, fraction of $sp^3$-hybridised carbons and stereocentre count, together with the composite multi-parameter optimization score and the synthetic-accessibility estimate. For the Set-C cohort these summaries locate the panel in a compact, moderately polar region dominated by natural-product cores, and the values are reported with the results. Two boundaries attach to this characterisation. The descriptors describe the sources from which the panel was drawn and carry no ethnobotanical validation of any candidate; and the summary statistics are cohort-conditional, so they should not be extrapolated to the parent library from which the cohort was filtered.

\subsection{Computational environment}

\subsection{Partition families and cross-validation}

Rigorous evaluation of predictive models requires careful partitioning of the data, and the choice of partition determines what the resulting performance estimate means. Four partition families are used across the two representation benchmarks. A stratified random split provides the in-domain baseline. A canonical scaffold split based on Bemis--Murcko ring-core separation enforces structural extrapolation by assigning molecules that share a scaffold to the same side of the split. A Butina cluster split based on ECFP4 Tanimoto distance at a similarity cutoff of $C = \num{0.55}$ enforces a coarser notion of chemical separation. Finally, a nearest-neighbour Tanimoto decile stratification orders test molecules by the similarity of their nearest training neighbour, so that performance can be resolved as a function of structural distance from the training distribution rather than reported as a single aggregate.

The learned-representation benchmark uses a repeated $5 \times 5$ cross-validation design, giving \num{25} fold--seed execution records per model architecture and partition family, and paired hypothesis testing on the five per-seed performance means. The descriptor benchmark uses 5-fold stratified cross-validation with fixed random seeds. Scaffold separation is confirmed independently by the decile analysis, because a model can be accurate within analogue series and still fail on unseen chemotypes; reporting both is necessary for a claim about prospective use. An exploratory partition at the level of scaffold clusters is used in the chemical-space analysis to verify that candidate sets remain disjoint.

\subsection{Performance metrics}

Performance metrics for binary classification include the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), which quantifies discrimination across all decision thresholds; the area under the precision--recall curve, which is more informative under class imbalance; the enrichment factor at a fixed early fraction of the ranked list; and the Matthews correlation coefficient, which is robust to imbalance. Accuracy and macro F1 are reported alongside ROC-AUC where they aid interpretation. For regression against docking scores, the coefficient of determination $R^{2}$ is reported under cross-validation. One metric requires explicit qualification: because the activity panels used in this thesis are highly enriched (approximately \qty{74}{\percent} active), the enrichment factor at \num{1} percent saturates at a prevalence-imposed ceiling and therefore cannot support a virtual-screening precision claim. Where that saturation occurs it is reported alongside the metric rather than omitted.

The saturation argument is stated formally because it recurs across the results. The enrichment factor at fraction $f$ of the ranked list is $\mathrm{EF}_f = p_f / \pi$, where $p_f$ is the active fraction within the top $f$ of the list and $\pi$ is the overall active prevalence. Its maximum value is bounded by $(1 - (1-f)(1-\pi)^{|L|}) / (f\pi)$ only for an ideal ranking; in the limit of a large ranked list the practical ceiling approaches $1/\pi$ times the top-fraction active rate, which cannot exceed \num{1}. At $\pi = \num{0.742}$ and $f = \num{0.01}$, a perfect ranking would place almost only actives in the top one percent, so $\mathrm{EF}_{1\%}$ collapses toward $1/\pi \approx \num{1.35}$ regardless of model quality, and observed values of \numrange{1.32}{1.35} reflect the panel composition rather than discriminative performance. The same reasoning bounds precision at the top of the list near the prevalence-imposed maximum. Enrichment-style metrics are therefore reported only for panels whose prevalence leaves them identifiable, and on the enriched panels they are reported with their ceilings attached.

\subsection{Conformal calibration and the distance-aware triage rule}

Two calibration tools accompany the learned-representation benchmark, and both are declared as operational hypotheses rather than as validated procedures. The first is split-conformal prediction: a calibration split of the training data is scored, the nonconformity scores of the calibration split are ranked, and the quantile at level $\lceil (n_{\mathrm{cal}} + 1)(1 - \alpha) \rceil / n_{\mathrm{cal}}$ defines a threshold that yields prediction sets whose empirical coverage is guaranteed marginally at the target level $1 - \alpha$ under exchangeability. The calibration is applied per model arm, and the reported diagnostics are empirical coverage and mean set size, which trade off against each other: a rule that covers more must predict more ambiguity. The second tool is post-hoc isotonic recalibration: the model's predicted scores are mapped to calibrated probabilities through an isotonic regression fitted on the calibration split, and calibration error is re-evaluated after the mapping. Isotonic regression is chosen over parametric calibration because it imposes no shape assumption on the score-to-probability map, at the cost of requiring sufficient calibration mass in every score region; regions without calibration support retain their uncalibrated values and the support boundary is reported.

The distance-aware triage rule assembles these pieces into a single operational proposal that the results chapters evaluate descriptively. The rule routes each test molecule to a representation arm according to the Tanimoto similarity of its nearest training neighbour: molecules in the interpolation regime, where classical fingerprints retain their advantage, are scored by the fingerprint baseline, and molecules in the structural-extrapolation tail, where the fingerprint advantage narrows, are routed to the arm with the best tail performance. Its coverage and calibration diagnostics are computed on the same frozen panel as the benchmark, and its evaluation inherits every boundary declared for that benchmark; in particular, because the rule is proposed and evaluated on the same panel, it is a testable operational hypothesis for prospective screening and not a demonstrated screening procedure. Prospective evaluation on an independent, low-prevalence library is the experiment that would convert the hypothesis into a validated rule, and that experiment is reserved for future work.

\subsection{Statistical significance testing and multiplicity}

Comparisons between models require appropriate statistical testing. For paired comparisons on identical cross-validation folds, paired $t$-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are used, with the paired $t$-test applied to per-seed means where a repeated design is available. Because the number of comparisons is large, multiplicity is handled deliberately rather than opportunistically. The descriptor benchmark declares a single confirmatory family comprising the \num{12} tests that support the comparative claims on the primary panel, including the classical descriptors against ECFP4, the hybrid descriptor against ECFP4, the quantum kernel against a linear kernel, and the ECFP4-inclusive fusion arms, and applies Holm--Bonferroni adjustment across that family at a family-wise error rate of \num{0.05}. Analyses outside that family, including the external label-source-shift transfer and the secondary correlation analyses, are reported as exploratory with uncorrected p-values and effect sizes, and for the transfer analysis a variance-corrected resampled paired test is reported in addition to the naive fold-level test because the training sets overlap. The learned-representation benchmark applies Benjamini--Hochberg false-discovery-rate adjustment across the scaffold-split contrasts, reporting exact adjusted p-values rather than a single tail bound. Where a comparison fails to detect a difference, the result is stated as a failure to detect rather than as evidence of equivalence, since fold-level tests that share training data have limited degrees of freedom.

A parallel limitation governs every correlation reported in this thesis, and it is quantitative rather than rhetorical. With a cohort of $n$ observations, a two-sided Spearman test at significance level $\alpha$ reaches a given power only above a minimum detectable association; for the cohorts analysed here the boundary is material. At $n = \num{17}$, a two-sided test at $\alpha = \num{0.05}$ reaches \qty{80}{\percent} power only for $|\rho| \geq \num{0.62}$, and at the Bonferroni-corrected threshold the power to detect a moderate association of $\rho = \num{0.5}$ falls below \num{0.4}. The consequence is asymmetrical: significant findings in these cohorts are interpretable, but the absence of significance is not evidence of independence, and every null association in the results chapters is reported with this power boundary attached rather than as a demonstration of independence. Power considerations are computed before interpretation for every correlation family, and the detectable-effect boundary is stated wherever a null result carries interpretive weight.

\subsection{Uncertainty taxonomy and formal test mechanics}

The quantities reported in this thesis carry three kinds of uncertainty that are never interchanged. Sampling uncertainty is the frame-level or fold-level dispersion of an estimate within one execution of a declared protocol, and it is what a standard error or a cross-validated interval describes. Protocol sensitivity is the response of a derived quantity to a declared arbitrary choice, such as a threshold, a seed, a weight or a scoring function, and it is what seed-dispersion bounds, weight-sensitivity checks and cross-scoring agreements quantify. Replicate variance is the dispersion across independent executions of the same protocol on the same system, and it is the only one of the three that bears on the reproducibility of an endpoint estimate; it is available only where replicates exist. The distinction matters because the three are routinely conflated into a single error bar: a frame-level standard error says nothing about protocol sensitivity, and a protocol-sensitivity bound says nothing about replicate variance. Where replicate variance is unavailable, which is the case for the single-replicate pilot trajectories, the reported intervals are explicitly labelled as within-execution dispersions and no reproducibility claim is attached to them.

The formal machinery of each reported test is fixed rather than chosen per analysis. Spearman rank correlations are computed on paired observations with permutation-based null distributions, the permutation count fixed at \num{100000} with a fixed random seed, and multiplicity handled by the declared family structure of the analysis in question. Paired cross-validated comparisons use per-seed means as the unit of analysis, so that the test operates on the appropriate degrees of freedom rather than on pseudo-replicated fold records. Exact binomial tests are used for direction-agreement statistics against a \qty{50}{\percent} chance level. For a proportion estimated from a small number of matched comparisons, such as a direction-agreement fraction or a divergence fraction, the interval reported is the Wilson score interval rather than the Wald interval, because the Wald interval is known to under-cover near the boundaries of the parameter space, which is exactly where small-sample proportions typically fall; the Wilson interval maintains nominal coverage at small counts and never produces bounds outside $[0, 1]$. The count and the denominator are always reported alongside the interval, since an interval without its sample size invites over-reading. Bootstrap intervals are percentile intervals from a fixed resample count with a fixed seed, reported for descriptive effect sizes rather than for hypothesis tests. The seed of every stochastic procedure is part of the frozen manifest, so that every reported interval is exactly reproducible.

A final governance rule concerns hyperparameters and the order in which choices are frozen. Baseline configurations, cross-validation designs, threshold values and the candidate grid of every tuned comparator are fixed in the analysis plan before the corresponding comparative experiment is run, and the frozen values are recorded in the provenance manifest. Where a sensitivity analysis is run after the fact, it is labelled as such and its role is bounded: it tests the robustness of a declared choice, it does not replace the choice retroactively. This ordering matters because the alternative, selecting hyperparameters after observing cross-validated performance, converts the reported performance estimate into an optimistic bias whose magnitude cannot be quantified after the fact. Nested designs, in which inner cross-validation selects a hyperparameter and outer cross-validation evaluates it, are used wherever the tuned comparator is part of a confirmatory claim; where a design is not nested, the claim it supports is correspondingly limited and reported as exploratory.

\subsection{Honest-negative reporting and provenance documentation}

This thesis adopts a policy of honest-negative reporting, in which null results, negative findings and limitations are documented with the same rigour as positive results. Honest-negative reporting prevents publication bias, provides realistic expectations for future work, and supports scientific integrity. Null results, meaning cases where a method does not significantly outperform a well-optimised baseline, are reported alongside positive findings with the statistical test that supports the absence of a detectable difference. Two consequences of this policy appear throughout the thesis: quantum-inspired representations are not presented as advantageous when a matched classical comparator performs equivalently, and learned representations are not presented as superior when scaffold-separated evaluation reverses the ranking observed under random splitting.

The policy is implemented through three documentation practices that make the negative results auditable rather than merely stated. First, failed and excluded work is retained in the record with an explicit status, so that the denominator of every success statistic is visible; a trajectory that failed quality control and an analysis that detected nothing are both first-class entries. Second, every negative claim is accompanied by the analysis that would have detected the opposite, including its power boundary, so the reader can distinguish \emph{we found nothing} from \emph{nothing was detectable}. Third, the scope of every negative result is fixed to the panel, protocol and label source under which it was established, so that a negative result is never converted by phrasing into a universal one. These practices cost space and repetition, and they are retained deliberately: in a thesis whose central empirical finding is that several fashionable representations add nothing over a well-maintained baseline, the credibility of the negative results is itself a primary result, and it is earned by this documentation rather than asserted.

Provenance documentation ensures that every computational result can be traced to its source data, scripts, parameters and execution environment. For each analysis a provenance manifest records the input files with cryptographic hashes, the software versions and dependencies, the hyperparameters and configuration files, the random seeds used for every stochastic procedure, the computational resources employed, and the output file locations and hashes. Manifests are stored in version-controlled repositories alongside the analysis scripts, so that results can be reproduced from the recorded state rather than from descriptions. An additional rule applies to the activity labels used in the two representation benchmarks: the primary labels are machine-learning predictions from an external model rather than assay measurements, and the external transfer analysis uses a separately sourced label set. Both facts are recorded at every point where a performance number is reported, so that a model's ability to predict a model's output is never presented as biological activity prediction. Failed experiments, exploratory analyses and superseded results are retained and labelled rather than deleted.

For every computational study reported in this thesis, provenance manifests are maintained in the results directories of the underlying project folders. Each computational experiment is assigned a unique job identifier, and results are stored in versioned output directories named by job identifier and timestamp. The quantum machine learning programme, whose protocol is established in the part of this thesis that follows, will adopt the same provenance scheme; until canonical runs exist, its status remains not computed and no result from it appears in this thesis.

\subsection{External data and reference-set versioning}

Several inputs to the analyses described in this chapter are external resources that change on their own release cycles, and each one is therefore versioned as part of the frozen experimental setup rather than treated as a stable background. The docking benchmarks are fixed panel releases, so the panel identifier and download date are recorded in the manifests; the protein structures are specific PDB entries cited by accession, with the retrieval date recorded because remediation of deposited entries can alter coordinates without changing the accession. The reference sets behind the chemical-space descriptors are pinned with the same discipline: the approved-drug reference, the African natural-product reference set and the network database used for the polypharmacology score are each recorded with their release versions, and the network confidence threshold is part of the manifest rather than a default inherited from a tool. The activity-label model is pinned by model identifier and version, since a silent update of the underlying model would change every label in the panel and invalidate comparisons across runs.

The versioning rule is that no reported number may depend on an unversioned external resource. Where a resource cannot be pinned, for example a web service whose historical states are not archived, the dependence is declared and the affected analysis is labelled as non-reproducible at the level of that input, with the rest of its chain still frozen. This rule costs little at analysis time and prevents the most common irreproducibility in computational drug discovery: results that cannot be regenerated because a database quietly changed underneath them. The same discipline extends to in-house artefacts passed between analyses: a descriptor file consumed by a downstream model is hashed and referenced by hash, so that a stale or partially rewritten file is detectable rather than silently consumed.

\subsection{Reproducibility infrastructure and reporting boundaries}

The reproducibility chain runs from frozen inputs to reported numbers without manual intervention. Each analysis is a versioned script whose execution reads only the hashed inputs declared in its manifest, writes only to its versioned output directory, and emits the job identifier, software versions, random seeds and wall-clock resource usage into the output record. Re-running the script against the frozen inputs must reproduce the reported numbers bit-for-bit for deterministic stages and within the declared seed distribution for stochastic ones; any deviation is a defect to be investigated, not a value to be silently repaired. Where a stage fails, the failure propagates to the output record as an explicit status rather than as a gap, and downstream analyses either exclude the affected records with the exclusion documented or halt.

Reporting boundaries are attached to estimands rather than to chapters, so that a reader meeting a number out of context still meets its scope. Docking scores carry the boundary that they are score-retention statistics on anchored poses; trajectory endpoints carry the boundary that they are within-protocol contrasts at single-replicate scope; activity metrics carry the boundary that their labels are model predictions rather than assay measurements; and every correlation carries its cohort size and the power boundary derived from it. The boundaries are not caveats appended after the fact: each one is fixed when the corresponding quantity is defined, and the results chapters state them at the point where the quantity first appears. This convention costs space, but it removes the failure mode in which a number circulates with its scope stripped, which is how over-claiming enters a manuscript without any single sentence being false.

The final reporting convention is the claim ledger. Each results chapter closes by restating its central claims in a fixed four-way status vocabulary: \emph{supported}, meaning supported within the declared protocol and sampling limits; \emph{refuted}, meaning the declared protocol produced evidence against the claim; \emph{not supported}, meaning the analysis failed to detect the claimed effect at the declared power, which is not a refutation; and \emph{open}, meaning the claim cannot be decided computationally and requires prospective experiment. The statuses are scope-qualified by construction, so a claim supported in one cohort is not silently promoted to a general claim, and a hypothesis evaluated only descriptively is recorded as open rather than as supported. The ledger is a completeness device as much as a honesty device: it forces every central claim to be revisited after the analyses, so that a claim that quietly lost its evidential support during the work is surfaced rather than forgotten.

\section{Conclusion}

In this chapter, the computational framework of the thesis has been specified end to end. The construction of the hybrid library was detailed, together with its two expansion routes, the declared scoring terms of the prioritisation pipeline, the four-target docking protocol, its three-part validation, and the correction and audit of the transporter receptor. The resistance-resilience scoring framework was established with its estimand architecture, eligibility rule, class definitions, network and chemical-space descriptors, and retrospective genotype--phenotype confrontation. The molecular-dynamics protocol was described across its three cohorts, from force-field assignment through equilibration to quality control, endpoint estimation, and the interaction-fingerprint complement, together with the distance-ratio definition that links the dynamics to the docking layer.

The descriptor and modelling layers were then specified: the classical fingerprint family with its three encoding principles, the persistent-homology, tensor and quantum-inspired descriptors under a common conformer-generation governance, the kernel diagnostic hierarchy that separates geometry from performance, and the random-forest, graph and transformer architectures with their frozen configurations. The statistical framework closed the chapter with partition families, activity-label provenance, prevalence-bounded metrics, conformal calibration and the distance-aware triage rule, significance testing with its power boundaries, the Wilson interval for small-sample proportions, the uncertainty taxonomy, reproducibility infrastructure, external-data versioning, and the claim-ledger convention.

In summary, the protocols presented in this chapter furnish the frozen methodological substrate on which every result of the next chapter rests. Each quantity that appears in the results is defined here with its estimand, its uncertainty type and its reporting boundary, so that the results can be read as tests of the declared protocol rather than as a collection of numbers, and the comparison standard that the quantum machine learning programme must satisfy follows directly from this specification.
