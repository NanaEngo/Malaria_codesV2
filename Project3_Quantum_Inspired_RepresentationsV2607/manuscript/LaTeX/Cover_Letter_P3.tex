\opening{Dear Editors,}

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, \textbf{``Topological and tensor-network representations resolve chemical paradoxes in African antimalarial natural products''}, for consideration as an Original Research Article in the \textit{Journal of Cheminformatics}.

This work introduces three molecular representations---Topological Fingerprint (TFP), Tensor Network Embedding (TNE), and Quantum Kernel Score (QKS)---and benchmarks them against classical fingerprints on a library of 19{,}836 African natural-product-derived antimalarial candidates. Persistent homology resolves a scaffold paradox (92.6\% Tanimoto novelty with 69.3\% scaffold recovery) by separating H$_0$ (connectivity) from H$_1$ (ring topology). TNE achieves a 6.1$\times$ real-atom mean compression while retaining binding-relevant information. Quantum kernels, evaluated under a rigorously controlled protocol, are statistically indistinguishable from a properly tuned classical RBF baseline at all sample sizes (6-qubit QKS AUC 0.823 versus RBF 0.829 at $n=19{,}849$, $p=0.060$).

We report honest negative results: on the corrected canonical benchmark, classical ECFP4 remains the strongest single descriptor for primary screening (AUC 0.948). The hybrid descriptor (TFP+TNE+QK) achieves AUC 0.888, significantly below ECFP4 ($p<0.0001$) yet above every standalone quantum-inspired descriptor, with QKS as the principal positive contributor (ablation $\Delta=-0.040$). Cross-paper analysis on an expanded computational cohort ($n=494$) shows a weak unadjusted H$_1$--RRS association ($\rho=0.2399$) that disappears after adjustment for molecular size and related structural covariates ($\rho_{\mathrm{partial}}=0.0329$, $p=0.4668$); we present this as a size-confounding and reproducibility lesson, not as evidence of an independent biomarker.

Recent systematic reviews of quantum computing in bioinformatics and drug discovery have called for rigorous empirical evaluation of near-term quantum methods on real pharmaceutical datasets. Our reproducible benchmark answers this call with a fully specified protocol, public code and transparent reporting of both positive and negative findings. The ChEMBL panel is external to the eOS80CH label source; P3 does not claim molecule-level disjointness from the internal panel. We believe the work aligns closely with the scope of the \textit{Journal of Cheminformatics}: reusable open-source molecular descriptors, a carefully controlled benchmark, and an honest assessment of when quantum-inspired methods do not outperform classical baselines.

Code and machine-readable benchmark results are accessible to reviewers in the public repository (\url{https://github.com/NanaEngo/Malaria_codesV2}) under the MIT licence. This work is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and all authors have approved the submission.
