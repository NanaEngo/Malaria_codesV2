\section*{Introduction}

This supplementary document details methodological protocols, scoring metrics, and dataset statistics supporting the main text (Temgoua \textit{et al.}, 2026). The main manuscript describes a computational framework for antimalarial drug discovery that integrates African natural products with synthetic antimalarials, identifies antimalarial candidates (including select multi-target hits) through dual-filter consensus docking, and prioritizes leads using multi-parameter optimization scoring.

The supplementary content elaborates the Multi-Parameter Optimization (MPO) scoring framework introduced in the main manuscript (\cref{M-sec:mpo}), presenting mathematical formulations for the five desirability components and the polypharmacology bonus. Tabular summaries detail chemical space analysis, scaffold diversity, and drug-likeness characteristics of the \num{65856}-molecule hybrid library. Additional sections present generative model training and clustering validation, co-crystallized ligand classification for binding mode validation, computational resource comparisons, enrichment curves, UMAP projections of the latent space, and ScafVAE diversity benchmarks. Final tables provide target-specific docking outcomes, selectivity indices, and cytochrome P450 inhibition profiles for the \num{810} screened seed molecules with valid selectivity predictions against four \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} targets: dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR, PDB: 7F3Y), chloroquine resistance transporter (CRT, 6UKJ), ATPase 4 (ATP4, 9N10), and caseinolytic protease (ClpP, 4GM2).

All calculation results referenced in this supplementary material are archived in the project repository (\url{https://github.com/NanaEngo/Malaria_codesV2}) and can be regenerated using the scripts provided in the \texttt{scripts/} directory.

\section*{Multi-parameter optimization scoring function}

The MPO framework integrates five normalized desirability components into a composite score for hit prioritization. This approach extends the single-objective docking optimization strategy by simultaneously considering binding affinity, geometric confidence, drug-likeness, ADMET safety, and structural rule compliance. The methodology builds upon established multi-parameter optimization principles adapted for computational drug discovery applications \cite{Hughes2008, Gleeson2008}.

The total MPO score for compound $i$ against target $j$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{MPO}_{\text{total}, i} = \sum_{k=1}^{5} w_k \cdot S_{k,i} + \text{Polypharmacology Bonus}_i
\label{eq:mpo_total}
\end{equation}
where $w_k$ are the component weights (Vina \qty{35}{\percent}, DiffDock \qty{25}{\percent}, QED \qty{20}{\percent}, ADMET \qty{15}{\percent}, Ro5 \qty{5}{\percent}) and $S_{k,i}$ are the normalized desirability scores scaled to [\num{0}, \num{1}]. The polypharmacology bonus rewards compounds exhibiting binding against multiple targets simultaneously.

\subsubsection*{Weight selection rationale}

The component weights reflect established priorities in early-stage computational drug discovery \cite{Hughes2008, Gleeson2008}. The \qty{35}{\percent} weight assigned to Vina binding affinity prioritizes thermodynamic stability as the primary filter, consistent with structure-based drug design principles where binding affinity serves as the initial discriminator \cite{Trott2009}. DiffDock geometric confidence (\qty{25}{\percent}) provides complementary validation of pose quality \cite{Corso2023}, with the combined \qty{60}{\percent} weight for docking components reflecting the central role of target engagement in hit identification.

Drug-likeness (QED, \qty{20}{\percent}) receives substantial weight to ensure compounds satisfy oral bioavailability criteria \cite{Lipinski2004}, while ADMET safety (\qty{15}{\percent}) addresses early-stage toxicity concerns \cite{Swanson2024}. The modest \qty{5}{\percent} weight for Lipinski violations reflects that Ro5 compliance is already captured partially within QED, and modern drug discovery increasingly tolerates single violations for complex targets \cite{Hughes2008}. This weighting scheme balances potency, developability, and safety considerations typical of lead identification campaigns \cite{Gleeson2008}.

\subsection*{Component 1: Vina binding affinity ($S_{\text{vina}}$, \qty{35}{\percent})}

The Vina component is linearly scaled between the observed minimum and maximum binding affinities across all docked compounds:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{vina}, i} = \frac{\text{Vina}_i - \text{Vina}_{\min}}{\text{Vina}_{\max} - \text{Vina}_{\min}}
\label{eq:vina}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Vina}_i$ is the best binding affinity (\unit{\kilo\calorie\per\mole}) for compound $i$, and $\text{Vina}_{\min}$, $\text{Vina}_{\max}$ are the dataset bounds. More negative values yield higher desirability. This linear transformation preserves the rank ordering of compounds while mapping raw energies to a [\num{0}, \num{1}] scale compatible with other desirability components. Linear scaling was selected over exponential or power-law transformations to maintain proportional differences in binding affinity across the scoring range, consistent with the approximately linear relationship between binding free energy and experimental potency within the typical docking score range of \qtyrange{-10}{-5}{\kilo\calorie\per\mole}.

\subsection*{Component 2: DiffDock confidence ($S_{\text{diff}}$, \qty{25}{\percent})}

The DiffDock confidence score is transformed via a logistic sigmoid function to map structural log-odds to probabilistic desirability:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{diff}, i} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-c_i}}
\label{eq:diffdock}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is the DiffDock confidence score for compound $i$ \cite{Corso2023}. This transformation ensures that high-confidence poses ($c > \num{0}$) receive scores approaching \num{1.0}, while low-confidence poses ($c < \num{-1.5}$) receive scores approaching \num{0}. The sigmoidal mapping was chosen over linear scaling because DiffDock confidence scores follow a non-uniform distribution with a sharp transition around $c = \num{0}$, and the sigmoid function naturally captures this threshold behavior while providing smooth gradation for intermediate confidence values.

\subsection*{Component 3: Quantitative estimate of drug-likeness ($S_{\text{qed}}$, \qty{20}{\percent})}

The QED score is used directly as a desirability component without transformation:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{qed}, i} = \text{QED}_i
\label{eq:qed}
\end{equation}
where $\text{QED}_i \in [\num{0}, \num{1}]$ is the quantitative estimate of drug-likeness for compound $i$, computed from eight molecular properties using the RDKit implementation \cite{GregLandrum2025}. QED values $> \num{0.70}$ are considered indicative of drug-like compounds \cite{Lipinski2004}.

\subsection*{Component 4: ADMET safety profile ($S_{\text{admet}}$, \qty{15}{\percent})}

The ADMET component aggregates \num{11} critical pharmacokinetic and safety parameters into a composite desirability index:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{admet}, i} = \frac{1}{11} \sum_{m=1}^{11} d_m(\text{ADMET}_{i,m})
\label{eq:admet}
\end{equation}
where $d_m$ is the individual desirability function for ADMET endpoint $m$, predicted using ADMET-AI \cite{Swanson2024}. The \num{11} endpoints are:

\begin{itemize}
\item Oral bioavailability (human intestinal absorption, HIA)
\item Aqueous solubility ($\log S$)
\item Caco-2 permeability
\item Blood-brain barrier penetration
\item hERG liability (cardiotoxicity risk)
\item AMES mutagenicity
\item Hepatotoxicity (DILI)
\item Synthetic accessibility (SA score)
\item P-glycoprotein substrate status
\item Cytochrome P450 inhibition risk (composite of CYP2C9, 2C19, 3A4, 2D6)
\item Total clearance profile
\end{itemize}

Each endpoint is scored as a binary or continuous desirability function (\num{1} = favourable, \num{0} = unfavourable), with thresholds derived from FDA-approved drug distributions.

\subsection*{Component 5: Lipinski's rule of five penalty ($P_{\text{Ro5}}$, \qty{5}{\percent})}

A penalty term proportional to formal Lipinski's rule of five violations \cite{Lipinski2004}:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{Ro5}, i} = 1 - 0.2 \cdot n_{\text{violations}, i}
\label{eq:ro5}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{violations}, i}$ is the number of Ro5 violations (\numrange{0}{4}). The Ro5 criteria are: molecular weight $> \qty{500}{\dalton}$, $\log P > \num{5}$, hydrogen bond donors $> \num{5}$, and hydrogen bond acceptors $> \num{10}$. Penalties are: \num{0} violations = \num{1.0}, \num{1} violation = \num{0.8}, \num{2} violations = \num{0.6}, \num{3} violations = \num{0.4}, \num{4} violations = \num{0.2}.

\subsection*{Polypharmacology bonus}

Compounds exhibiting binding ($\text{MPO}_{\text{target}} \geq \num{0.50}$) against multiple targets receive an additional bonus to reward multi-target activity:
\begin{equation}
\text{Polypharmacology Bonus}_i = 0.05 \cdot (n_{\text{targets}, i} - 1) \quad \text{if } n_{\text{targets}, i} \geq 2
\label{eq:poly_bonus}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{targets}, i}$ is the number of targets against which compound $i$ achieves $\text{MPO}_{\text{target}} \geq \num{0.50}$. This bonus is capped at +0.15 (\num{4} targets). The \num{0.05} increment per additional target represents a modest computational preference for multi-target profiles that may be relevant to resistance hypotheses; it does not demonstrate resistance circumvention or therapeutic advantage. The $\text{MPO} \geq \num{0.50}$ threshold ensures that only compounds meeting minimum quality criteria across all scoring components qualify for the bonus, thereby distinguishing genuine multi-target candidates from non-selective binders with poor drug-likeness or safety profiles.

\subsection*{Centroid-based screening and MPO stratification}

The MPO framework was applied within a centroid-based virtual screening workflow to enable efficient exploration of the \num{65856}-molecule library. Rather than exhaustively docking all compounds, the workflow proceeded as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{K-Means clustering:} The library was partitioned into \num{484} clusters based on \num{64}-dimensional VAE latent representations, with one centroid selected per cluster to represent its chemical neighborhood.
\item \textbf{Consensus docking:} All \num{484} centroids were docked against four \textit{P. falciparum} targets (PfDHFR, PfCRT, PfATP4, PfClpP) using dual-filter consensus scoring (AutoDock Vina + DiffDock). Centroids were classified as EXCELLENT, GOOD, or OTHERS based on binding affinity and geometric confidence thresholds. Of the \num{1936} centroid--target Vina records, \num{71} (\qty{3.7}{\percent}) yielded no usable pose: \num{67} returned no valid pose and \num{4} produced non-negative (physically implausible) scores, with per-target no-pose counts of PfDHFR \num{33}, PfCRT \num{17}, PfClpP \num{16}, and PfATP4 \num{1}. Failed records were excluded from consensus scoring and MPO calculation, so per-target denominators reflect valid poses only.
\item \textbf{MPO scoring:} \num{93} centroids passing consensus classification (EXCELLENT or GOOD) received full MPO evaluation across all five desirability components (Vina, DiffDock, QED, ADMET, Ro5).
\item \textbf{Optimization-worthy threshold:} \num{76} centroids achieved $\text{MPO} \geq \num{0.40}$, defining the optimization-worthy tier. Within this tier, \num{7} centroids achieved $\text{MPO} \geq \num{0.50}$ (secondary hits), while \num{69} centroids fell in the \numrange{0.40}{0.50} range (promising scaffolds).
\item \textbf{Scaffold expansion:} The \num{76} optimization-worthy centroids map to \num{53} unique clusters (multiple centroids can represent the same cluster because centroids are selected as representative molecules rather than cluster identifiers). Scaffold expansion from these \num{53} clusters yielded \num{40481} molecules (mean cluster size: \num{763.8}) for downstream lead optimization. Of these, \num{19913} molecules satisfied the final synthetic accessibility and MPO criteria, defining the final set of synthesizable leads.
\end{enumerate}

This centroid-based strategy reduced computational cost by \qty{99.3}{\percent} compared to exhaustive library-scale docking (\Cref{tab:computational_comparison}) while preserving chemical diversity through cluster-based sampling. The $\text{MPO} \geq \num{0.40}$ threshold was selected to balance stringency with practical hit list size, ensuring that expanded clusters represent chemically tractable starting points for medicinal chemistry optimization.

\subsection*{Natural product relatedness filtering}

To identify candidates retaining structural features of African natural products, NP-relatedness was assessed using Tanimoto similarity to \num{396} seed African NPs. Similarity was computed using Morgan fingerprints (radius \num{2}, \num{2048} bits) \cite{Rogers2010}, with compounds achieving maximum Tanimoto $\geq \num{0.4}$ to any seed NP classified as NP-related. This threshold balances scaffold conservation with sufficient structural diversity for lead optimization.

Among the \num{76} optimization-worthy centroids, \num{13} (\qty{17.1}{\percent}) satisfied the NP-relatedness criterion (Tanimoto $\geq \num{0.4}$), representing candidates that combine favorable multi-target profiles with natural product-inspired scaffolds. The remaining \num{63} centroids (\qty{82.9}{\percent}) represent structurally distinct analogues with Tanimoto $< \num{0.4}$, confirming that generative expansion explored new chemical space while preserving drug-like characteristics.

This dual population enables two complementary lead optimization strategies: (1) NP-related candidates ($n = \num{13}$) use scaffolds with prior bioactivity annotations; any toxicity or ADMET advantage remains to be tested experimentally; (2) structurally new candidates ($n = \num{63}$) offer opportunities for chemical exploration and can be evaluated in future resistance panels; they do not yet demonstrate circumvention of existing resistance mechanisms. The NP-relatedness filter was applied post-MPO scoring to avoid biasing the optimization framework toward natural product-like structures, ensuring that both populations met stringent multi-parameter quality criteria.

\subsection*{Framework limitations and sensitivity}

\label{sec:mpo_sensitivity_methods}

\textbf{Sensitivity methodology.} The five MPO component scores comprise S$_{\text{vina}}$ (Vina binding affinity), S$_{\text{diff}}$ (DiffDock confidence), S$_{\text{qed}}$ (QED drug-likeness), S$_{\text{admet}}$ (ADMET composite), and P$_{\text{Ro5}}$ (Lipinski penalty). Because individual Vina and DiffDock scores are not stored per molecule in the c6 library, S$_{\text{vina}}$ and S$_{\text{diff}}$ are back-calculated from the shared residual of the weighted MPO score after subtracting the QED, ADMET, and Ro5 contributions, then split proportionally (35:25 ratio). Weight perturbations of Vina and DiffDock therefore reallocate within this combined residual rather than varying the two components independently. QED, ADMET, and Ro5 perturbations reflect true independent variation.

The MPO framework represents a pragmatic balance between computational tractability and multi-objective optimization. Component weights were selected based on established drug discovery priorities \cite{Hughes2008, Lipinski2004} rather than target-specific optimization, which would require experimental validation data currently unavailable for the majority of the library. All docking CSVs report provenance fields (target, exhaustiveness, grid version) so that every score can be traced to the receptor preparation and Vina search depth used. The framework's primary limitation is the dataset-dependent nature of min-max scaling for Vina scores, which precludes direct score comparison across different compound libraries without renormalization.

 Formal sensitivity analysis across \num{25} weight perturbations ($\pm \qtyrange{10}{20}{\percent}$ per component) yielded mean Spearman rank correlation $\rho = \num{0.792}$ for the top-1,000 compounds and mean Jaccard similarity of \num{0.548} for the top-20 hit list, indicating moderate sensitivity to weight choice (\cref{tab:sm_s17_mpo_sensitivity}). When both stability criteria (top-1000 $\rho > \num{0.95}$ and top-20 Jaccard $\geq \num{0.70}$) are required, only \qty{5}{\percent} of non-baseline perturbations passed (DiffDock \qty{25}{\percent}; Vina, QED, ADMET, and Ro5 \qty{0}{\percent}), confirming that the ranking is moderately rather than strongly robust to weight choice; a substantial fraction of perturbations did not pass the stability test. The $\text{MPO} \geq \num{0.40}$ threshold for optimization-worthy centroids was selected to balance stringency with practical cluster expansion size, yielding \num{76} centroids that map to \num{53} unique clusters and expand to \num{40481} molecules (\num{19913} final synthesizable leads) representing chemically tractable starting points for medicinal chemistry optimization.

\subsection*{Evaluation of clustering strategies and dimensionality}

\subsubsection*{Bootstrap confidence interval methodology}

Bootstrap confidence intervals were calculated using \num{1000} resamples with replacement. For each resample:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Randomly sample $N$ compounds (with replacement) from the validation set, where $N$ is the original dataset size
  \item Calculate ROC-AUC, EF\qty{5}{\percent}, EF\qty{10}{\percent}, and BEDROC for the resample
  \item Store the metric values
\end{enumerate}

The \qty{95}{\percent} confidence interval was determined as the \num{2.5}th and \num{97.5}th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. Narrow confidence intervals indicate consistent performance across different dataset compositions.

\subsubsection*{Enrichment metric definitions}

\textbf{ROC-AUC (Receiver Operating Characteristic - Area Under Curve):}
Measures the probability that a randomly chosen active compound ranks higher than a randomly chosen decoy. Range: [0, 1], where 1.0 indicates perfect separation and 0.5 indicates random ranking. ROC-AUC is threshold-independent and provides an overall assessment of ranking quality.

\textbf{Enrichment Factor at \qty{5}{\percent} (EF\qty{5}{\percent}):}
Quantifies how many times more actives are found in the top \qty{5}{\percent} of the ranked list compared to random selection. EF\qty{5}{\percent} = \num{20} indicates perfect early enrichment (all actives in top \qty{5}{\percent}). This metric is critical for experimental validation where only top-ranked compounds are typically tested.

\textbf{BEDROC (Boltzmann-Enhanced Discrimination of ROC, $\alpha = \num{20}$):}
Emphasizes early recognition by exponentially weighting top-ranked compounds. Higher values indicate better prioritization of actives in experimentally relevant top-ranked positions. BEDROC addresses the "early recognition" problem where standard ROC-AUC may not adequately reflect performance in the critical top-ranked region.

\textbf{Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC):}
Assesses performance with imbalanced datasets (common in drug discovery where actives are rare). PR-AUC is more informative than ROC-AUC for datasets with few actives relative to decoys, as it focuses on the positive class (actives) rather than treating both classes equally.

\subsection*{Validation results - detailed analysis}

The enrichment validation demonstrated excellent discriminative performance across all four targets, with ROC-AUC values ranging from \numrange{0.924}{1.000}. Bootstrap confidence intervals (\num{1000} resamples) confirmed statistical confidence, with narrow CIs indicating consistent performance across different dataset compositions.

\textbf{PfDHFR (7F3Y):} Achieved ROC-AUC of \num{0.924} [\numrange{0.895}{0.951}] against the challenging DEKOIS benchmark, demonstrating reliable discrimination between \num{132} actives and \num{267} carefully matched decoys. The EF\qty{5}{\percent} of \num{2.57} indicates that actives are enriched \num{2.57}-fold in the top \qty{5}{\percent} of ranked compounds, confirming effective early recognition.

\textbf{PfATP4 (9N10) and PfClpP (4GM2):} Both achieved perfect separation (ROC-AUC = \num{1.000}) using score-based classification, demonstrating that consensus scoring effectively distinguishes high-scoring from low-scoring MMV compounds for these targets.

\textbf{PfCRT (6UKJ):} Achieved ROC-AUC of \num{0.971} [\numrange{0.949}{0.989}] with a high BEDROC of \num{9.434}, reflecting the dataset composition (\num{359} actives, \num{40} decoys, \qty{90}{\percent} active rate) where most MMV compounds dock favorably to this target.

These results document the evaluated computational workflow for antimalarial candidate prioritization and support testing dual-consensus scoring as a screening filter; independent inactive panels are required to quantify false-positive reduction.
